SunnySideUp: Product Brief

One-line pitch

SunnySideUp gives UK homeowners, renters, and leaseholders clear, independent solar viability checks to help them take confident climate action.

Problem

Solar quotes are hard to compare. Installers vary system-size definitions, included services like scaffolding, DNO paperwork, or battery bundling, and financing terms. Because of this, two quotes for a similar job can differ by thousands of pounds with no obvious reason why.[^12] No one in the current market has an incentive to fix that. Installer pricing opacity is a documented regulatory concern: the CMA's own 2022 investigation into this sector specifically flagged "inaccurate and unrealistic headline price information."[^1] Every existing UK solar comparison platform found in research runs a lead-generation or referral-fee model. These tools receive payment per lead, which encourages them to maximize quotes and sales instead of providing clear, direct comparisons.[^2] SunnySideUp avoids this conflict of interest entirely.

Sales pressure comes from that same gap. A "Which?" survey of solar-panel owners found more than a third had been cold-called with false claims (mandatory "government-required" checks, fire-risk warnings, unsolicited battery upsells), and the CMA and Ofgem have both run consumer-protection investigations into the sector.[^1]

The market has also just shifted underneath this problem, in a way that's confusing without an independent explainer. BS 7671 Amendment 4 (in force 15 April 2026) legalized hard-wired plug-in solar installs by a registered electrician. True DIY self-install, where a buyer purchases a kit and plugs it directly into a wall socket, becomes legal 27 August 2026 under SI 2026/848, the regulations DESNZ made following its consultation response.[^3] A renter looking at a £500 kit today needs clear, impartial guidance to understand the difference between an electrician installation, legal now, and a DIY setup that isn't legal until that date.

This sales environment, lack of transparency, and shifting regulations feed directly into consumer frustration. Many UK residents want to lower their carbon footprint, but confusing advice, high upfront costs, and predatory sales tactics leave them feeling anxious and unable to take meaningful climate action.[^11]

Who it's for

Two segments, defined by roof access:

  • Roof-access homeowners considering rooftop solar. This is the segment most exposed to direct sales pressure, including cold calls and in-home sales pitches.
  • No-roof-access renters and leaseholders considering plug-in or balcony solar. Leaseholders own equity but generally still need freeholder or management-company consent to alter a building's exterior.[^4] This is a newly-legal, thinly-served category with almost no independent guidance today.

Both segments use the product at one of two moments:

  1. Before any sales conversation, to set a baseline.
  2. After receiving a quote, to get an unbiased second opinion.

Why now

The plug-in solar category is mid-transition. BS 7671 Amendment 4 (15 April 2026) legalized electrician-installed plug-in solar. DESNZ's consultation on the consumer self-install product standard closed 30 June 2026, and its response, published 16 July 2026, confirmed the government is proceeding: the enabling regulations (SI 2026/848) are made and come into force 27 August 2026, the date true DIY self-install becomes legal.[^3] Between now and then is the window where an independent explainer is most valuable: self-install is legally confirmed but not yet in force, most consumers (and most existing solar sites) haven't caught up to the nuance, and a renter buying a kit today still needs an electrician to install it legally. Separately, 2025 was UK solar's record installation year with 31–37% growth,[^5] so rooftop demand, and the sales pressure that comes with it, is already at an all-time high.

Market size & opportunity

UK solar installations passed 2 million by March 2026, following a record 2025.[^5] No adoption data exists yet for plug-in or balcony solar specifically; the category is too new to measure. The closest comparable-market signal is Germany's Balkonkraftwerke market, which moved from roughly 270,000 registered units in 2023 to over 1 million by mid-2025, with growth accelerating sharply after German renters gained an explicit legal right to install in 2024. UK renters are still waiting on that same clarity.[^5][^10]

While no single figure captures the total addressable opportunity,[^7] two signals suggest it's substantial:

  1. The UK government's 2025 Solar Roadmap targets 45–47GW of installed solar capacity by 2030, up from about 19GW in May 2025, roughly 2.5x growth in five years, with rooftop installations specifically prioritized.[^9]
  2. Combining ONS data on renters (about 8.8 million private and social renting households) with MHCLG's leaseholder statistics (about 2.6 million owner-occupied leasehold homes) indicates roughly 11 million English households in the plug-in PV category.[^8]

Additionally, the opportunity isn't purely financial: UK surveys consistently show a gap between climate concern and felt ability to act, with one 2025 survey finding 93% of respondents felt some degree of eco-anxiety, many describing frustration at caring about climate change while having no way to act on it.[^11]

Competitive landscape & differentiation

Every UK solar comparison platform identified in research (CompareMySolar, Uswitch's solar comparison, Solar Selections, Easy PV, Renewable Energy Hub, Solar Planet, Solar PV Quotes Compare) runs a lead-generation or referral-fee model.[^2] None were found to be structurally independent of installer payment. The closest real analog to SunnySideUp's positioning is Energy Saving Trust, a UK body funded by government and private sources providing impartial advice.[^2] That's evidence an independently funded model can actually work in this space.

Even Energy Saving Trust leaves a real gap. It doesn't sell or install anything itself, and its own solar calculator uses generic assumptions before telling users to check with their installer for a more accurate number.[^2] That final, personalized figure gets handed back to the same installer whose headline pricing the CMA has already flagged as often inaccurate.[^1] SunnySideUp gives that specific, personalized read directly, without sending the user back to the party being evaluated.

Solution

A fast, plain-English viability check for rooftop and plug-in solar. Users enter a postcode and a few quick details to see where their property sits on a simple green-amber-red scale within seconds.

The tool provides an immediate, transparent read on potential energy savings, financial return, and legal requirements without pushing a sale or harvesting user data for installer leads. If solar does not make financial or practical sense, the system says so directly.

Hypothesis

We believe that UK consumers will use an independent, non-referral-funded check to evaluate rooftop sales pitches and plug-in solar options.

We will test this through four specific user behaviors:

  • Pre-purchase verification. Homeowners mid-pitch or renters exploring plug-in kits use the tool to check financial and legal viability before making a purchase.
  • Validation of unviable results. When users receive an unviable outcome ("Red"), they express high trust through exit feedback ("This saved me time/money") and accept the answer rather than seeking a second opinion from a pushy installer.
  • Organic word-of-mouth. Users who receive an unviable result share the tool with friends or family facing similar sales pressure, treating SunnySideUp as a consumer protection resource.
  • Institutional sponsorship. Public bodies or innovation funds (such as Ofgem SIF) agree to fund the platform, validating that an independent advice tool aligns with national clean-energy goals.

Go-to-market

SunnySideUp relies on low-cost, trust-based acquisition channels targeting users at two critical decision moments: before researching solar, or immediately after receiving a quote or pitch.

1. Channel strategy by user segment

Roof-access homeowners (the "second opinion" flow) - Search intent (SEO): capture high-intent queries driven by quote confusion (e.g. "Is [Installer Name] quote fair," "How many solar panels do I actually need UK," "Solar panel quote sanity check"). - Consumer protection partnerships: partner with consumer advocacy groups, local energy advice hubs, and community energy groups (e.g. MoneySavingExpert forum threads, Which? community discussions) as the recommended independent quote checker. - Organic social & local groups: share real-world quote teardowns in local Facebook/Nextdoor groups where homeowners routinely ask neighbors, "Has anyone used this solar company?"

Renters & leaseholders (the "can I do this?" flow) - Content & publisher partnerships: seed plain-English guides on tenant rights and balcony solar rules with renter advocacy groups (e.g. Generation Rent, Shelter advisory hubs) and eco-living publishers. - Direct-to-community distribution: reach leaseholders through flat-owner forums, leasehold advisory networks, and Reddit communities (r/UKPersonalFinance, r/HousingUK).

2. Strategic institutional distribution

Instead of paying for ads, SunnySideUp distributes through institutional partners who want to protect consumers or hit decarbonization targets without building their own software:

  • Local authorities & housing associations: provided as a free digital tool in municipal climate-action portals and tenant handbooks.
  • Distribution Network Operators (DNOs): offered via DNO customer portals.

3. Business model & sustainability (distinct from distribution)

The plan is for core operations to be funded through non-conflicted public interest sources, such as the Ofgem Strategic Innovation Fund (SIF), the Network Innovation Allowance (NIA), or climate-focused grants,[^6] preserving independence from installer lead-generation fees. This is a hypothesis, not a secured funding arrangement (see Critical success factors).

Success metrics & validation methodology

Leading indicators - Completed checks by segment: volume of completed checks split by rooftop vs. plug-in users. - Exit micro-feedback on unviable results: percentage of users receiving a Red ("unviable") outcome who confirm value on exit ("This saved me time/money"). - Organic referral rate: percentage of users who share their check results or link the tool to others after completing a check.

Lagging indicators - Multi-stage engagement: homeowners who complete an initial "before" check returning later to run an "after" second-opinion check on a specific installer quote. - Organic advocacy & coverage: citations or direct embeds of the tool within consumer advocacy forums, local energy advice hubs, and tenant rights platforms.

Go/no-go test A concept test, not a literal Sean Ellis PMF survey, since that methodology is a retention test built for people with actual usage history, and no one has used SunnySideUp yet. In the synthetic-interview pass (next in sequence), each persona is first shown a short description of SunnySideUp (the one-line pitch plus Solution) within the interview itself, then asked how disappointed they'd be if that described concept were no longer available to them, rather than being asked cold about a product they've never experienced. The classic 40% "very disappointed" threshold was calibrated on real usage data (Dropbox, Eventbrite, LogMeIn), so it's a directional benchmark here, not equivalent-strength evidence: treat any resulting percentage as a proxy for concept resonance, to be re-tested for real once a live prototype exists.

Riskiest-assumption signals (from scope.md, distinct from the four Hypothesis behaviors above: these are the underlying risks the whole hypothesis rests on, not the behaviors it predicts): 1. Renters/leaseholders will actively seek an independent check before buying, rather than trusting a marketplace listing or installer → signal: synthetic interviews show this as a real, named step in their process. 2. Homeowners mid-sales-conversation will pause for a second opinion, rather than just accepting or rejecting the number they were given → signal: synthetic interviews surface this as plausible persona behavior consistent with their own reasoning. 3. A non-conflicted revenue model exists along the lines named in Go-to-market → signal: a specific, named funding mechanism is identified as a plausible fit at the roadmap stage. 4. Plug-in solar's UK regulatory status stays stable enough, near-term, for product guidance about it not to go stale within the life of this case study → signal: the self-install rules land as currently expected (SI 2026/848 in force 27 August 2026), without another material change before then.

Critical success factors

  • DIY self-install needs to actually go live on schedule. The regulations legalizing it (SI 2026/848) are made and dated, coming into force 27 August 2026, so this risk has substantially de-risked since the original draft, but the in-force date hasn't arrived yet as of this brief, and whether certified products are actually available at launch is unconfirmed. If it slips, the plug-in segment's pitch shrinks to "get an electrician to install a £500 kit," a materially less compelling product.
  • A funding partner needs to exist that's willing to fund independent consumer advice without a referral or commission structure attached. Go-to-market names SIF, NIA, and climate grants as plausible fits, but none has been confirmed as an actual precedent for this exact product type. This is still a hypothesis, not a validated plan.
  • The institutional distribution channels named in Go-to-market (local authorities, housing associations, DNOs) need to actually agree to carry a third-party tool in their own portals or handbooks. This partnership model hasn't been researched against any real precedent yet; it's a plausible channel, not a confirmed one.
  • The "honest no" has to be defensible with real payback/viability math. This depends on the technical/economic grounding, which grounding-research.md itself flags as its weakest-sourced section (no official UK payback dataset exists).

Open questions

  • Which specific funding mechanism (SIF, NIA, Innovate UK, Nesta, or something else) actually fits: to be researched at the roadmap stage.
  • Whether renters genuinely need landlord permission for a plug-in kit under their own tenancy agreement: the Renters' Rights Act 2025's actual text, verified directly, contains no provision on solar or alterations at all, so it doesn't grant the "zero permission" claim some commercially-motivated sources make (grounding-research.md §Leasehold and tenancy consent requirements). That closes the Act-specific question, but the underlying one, what an individual tenancy agreement or general landlord-tenant law requires, is separate and still open; don't state either "yes permission needed" or "no permission needed" to a real user without researching that directly.
  • How the two use-timings (before/after sales conversation) map to product flow: one flow with a branch, or two distinct entry points.
  • What data the calculator itself actually needs, and where each input comes from. This brief describes the output (a green/amber/red read in seconds) but not the data model behind it. grounding-research.md's Payback period section already names self-consumption rate, occupancy, export tariff, and region as the factors that swing the answer[^13] — but nothing yet says how the tool collects them from a real user. Unresolved inputs, in rough order of how much they affect the answer:
  • Household electricity consumption (kWh/year): drives the self-consumption estimate. Self-reported, a bill upload, or a smart-meter API pull are all plausible; none has been decided.
  • Gas vs. electric consumption split / heating type: not addressed anywhere in research yet. A household already on a heat pump or electric hot water has a materially different self-consumption case than a gas-heated one, and this shifts over time as electrification spreads.
  • Occupancy pattern (home during the day vs. out at work): named as a payback sensitivity factor in grounding-research.md but never turned into an actual input question.
  • Current tariff type and rate (price-cap standard variable vs. fixed vs. time-of-use): payback depends on which one the household is actually on, and SEG/export rates are supplier-specific and change roughly monthly per the same document.
  • Roof orientation/pitch (rooftop) or available socket location and sun exposure (plug-in): self-reported, since structural/shade assessment is explicitly out of scope per the case study's planned Limitations section.
  • Financing assumption: whether the payback estimate assumes a cash purchase or accounts for financing costs at all.

None of these has a stated source yet, and several (consumption baseline, occupancy, tariff) directly drive the payback number Critical success factors already flags as this brief's weakest-sourced part. Worth its own resolution pass at the roadmap/prototype stage, but the gap belongs here now rather than staying implicit.

Recommendation

GO, with one remaining explicit condition. The technical/economic grounding (payback math, plug-in generation estimates) needs to get materially firmer than what currently exists; that's the weakest-sourced part of the research today, and the "honest viability check" this product promises depends on it. The renter-permission condition from the prior draft is now partly resolved: the Renters' Rights Act's actual text, checked directly, contains no provision on solar or alterations, so it doesn't support the "no permission needed" claim some sources made. What it doesn't resolve, whether an individual tenancy agreement requires permission, stays in Open Questions rather than as a precondition to proceeding, since it's a matter of general contract/landlord-tenant law rather than something a single Act could have settled either way. The rooftop segment's grounding is solid enough to proceed without additional conditions. Proceed to personas and synthetic interviews next, as scoped, and treat the remaining condition as an active follow-up to close, not a caveat to carry indefinitely.
