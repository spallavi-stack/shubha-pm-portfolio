# Flexy Funnel Analysis & Experiment Design — Grounding Research

## Starting point (already established elsewhere, not re-researched here)

The funnel itself is frozen in two existing docs and is not reopened by this research: **invited → app download → EV connected → smart charging activated** (`product-brief.md`, `utility-dashboard-kpi-framework.md`). `product-brief.md`'s three PMF signals — pilot extension, downloads, smart charging activations — are the outcome metrics this funnel feeds.

## Stage 1: Invited → App download

**[Fact]** Uplight, a demand-response/marketplace software vendor serving utilities, states on its own marketing page: "We've seen 65% enrollment rates at utilities that implement Uplight's marketplace and DR programs together." (Source: [Uplight, Demand Response Solutions](https://uplight.com/solutions/demand-response/), accessed 2026-08-01.) No denominator (mailing list size, eligible customer base, etc.) or methodology is disclosed — this is a vendor's own marketing claim, not an audited or third-party-verified figure.

**[Fact]** ComEd's own EV EMS Pilot (run with WeaveGrid, Emporia, and Treehouse, executive summary released December 2025) enrolled twelve residential participants total. The pilot's public materials don't disclose how many households were invited to reach that number, so no invited→enrolled conversion rate exists for ComEd's own closest real precedent. (Source: `utility-offer-grounding-research.md` §A3, direct primary-source read of the pilot's executive-summary PDF.)

**[Inference]** A utility-endorsed invitation (email, bill insert, or app prompt from ComEd itself) is a trusted-institution, owned-channel touchpoint, closer in kind to an employer-benefits enrollment push or a bank's in-app cross-sell than to a cold-acquisition funnel. Lenny's 500+ product activation-rate survey doesn't cover this channel type directly (it's an activation-rate survey, not an invite-to-download survey), so this stage has no directly transferable benchmark from that source. Reasoning from the utility-channel trust literature that does exist (Uplight's 65% figure, however unaudited) and general owned-channel conversion patterns being meaningfully higher than paid acquisition: a plausible download-conversion range sits well above typical app-store cold-install rates.

**[Assumption]** For planning purposes, this pass assumes a 15–30% invited-to-download conversion range for Flexy's ComEd rollout — bounded below by "an owned-channel trusted invite should convert meaningfully better than cold outreach" and above by "Uplight's 65% figure is vendor-marketing-sourced with an undisclosed denominator, so treating it as an upper bound rather than an expectation is the conservative read." This is the single lowest-confidence number in this document and should be the first one replaced with real data once ComEd distribution actually happens.

## Stage 2: App download → EV connected

**[Fact]** Smartcar (the vehicle-data aggregator Flexy's own roadmap names as its integration approach) states on its own blog that customers most commonly drop off during vehicle-connect OAuth flows for three named reasons: uncertainty about whether their vehicle is a "connected car," data-privacy concerns, and unclear instructions. (Source: [Smartcar, "How mobility apps onboard vehicles with Smartcar Connect"](https://smartcar.com/blog/smartcar-connect-vehicle-onboarding), accessed 2026-08-01.) No quantified drop-off rate or completion percentage is disclosed anywhere on the page — this is qualitative, not a benchmark.

**[Fact]** Lenny's 500+ product activation-rate survey found "adding several authentication providers," "reducing friction of the connection/setup process," and "providing alternative ways to connect an account" among the most frequently cited tactics that improved activation rates — i.e., third-party account-connection is a commonly-named friction point across product categories generally, not unique to EV apps. (Source: [Lenny's Newsletter, "What is a good activation rate," 2022-10-25](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-activation-rate).)

**[Inference]** None of the four personas in `synthetic-interviews.md` expressed hesitation about the *concept* of connecting an EV account specifically — all four reacted positively to smart EV charging in Q4. The named trust barrier (Marcus, Sam, Jenna in Q6) is specifically about the car not being ready after automation is turned on, not about the account-linking step itself. This suggests Stage 2's drop-off risk is more a solvable UX problem (matching Smartcar's own named reasons: clarity, privacy reassurance, instructions) than a deep trust problem, and is likely Flexy's second-lowest-risk stage, not its biggest one.

## Stage 3: EV connected → Smart charging activated

**[Fact]** In Lenny's activation-rate survey, marketplace and e-commerce products had the lowest activation rates of the eight product categories measured, because their activation milestones typically require the highest-commitment action available (a first transaction, i.e. spending money), versus B2C freemium products' highest rates, which come from low-friction milestones (starting a session, logging one item). (Source: same survey as above.)

**[Inference]** Turning on smart charging automation is Flexy's highest-commitment action, not because money changes hands but because it hands physical control of a vehicle's charging schedule to the app — structurally closer to a marketplace's "first transaction" commitment level than to a SaaS product's typical low-friction activation milestone (viewing a dashboard, connecting a read-only data feed). On that basis, this stage should be modeled toward the low end of Lenny's overall distribution (25% median, 34% average, all product types) rather than the SaaS-specific band (30% median, 36% average) — SaaS's typical activation actions don't carry this kind of physical-control handoff.

**[Fact]** Three of the four personas whose Q4 reaction to smart EV charging was positive (Marcus, Jenna, Sam — Q6 in `synthetic-interviews.md`) independently named the same specific fear when asked what would make them stop trusting or turn off the automation: the car not being ready when actually needed. This is a direct citation to already-completed portfolio research, not new data.

**[Inference]** This is Flexy's highest drop-off risk stage on two independent, converging signals: a general product-category pattern (high-commitment activation actions convert lowest across a 500+ product survey) and Flexy-specific qualitative evidence (the one named trust-breaker, raised unprompted by three of four relevant personas, is specifically about this stage's core action). Both signals point the same direction, which is stronger grounding than either alone.

## What the closest competitive analogs don't disclose

Checked ComEd's own EV EMS pilot materials, Optiwatt's public marketing (`utility-offer-grounding-research.md` already sourced both for the utility-offer work) and Uplight's marketing page specifically for funnel/conversion data, not just product existence:

- **ComEd's own EV EMS pilot** (WeaveGrid/Emporia/Treehouse, Dec 2025 executive summary) reports only a final enrolled count (twelve participants) — no invited count, no stage-by-stage funnel, no activation rate for the panel-management feature it did ship.
- **Optiwatt** discloses aggregate lifetime totals (31,224+ enrolled devices, 44 programs, 36 utility partners, 1.3+ GWh shifted) but no per-program enrollment or activation rate, and no funnel breakdown between "device enrolled" and "actively managed."
- **Uplight** discloses exactly one number (the 65% figure above) with no denominator, no stage breakdown, and no distinction between download, connection, and activation.

None of the three closest real-world analogs to Flexy's funnel publish anything resembling a stage-by-stage conversion breakdown. This is itself a finding worth stating plainly in the eventual public doc: there is no real precedent to benchmark against directly, which is exactly why this document leans on general product-activation research (Lenny's survey) and the persona/interview evidence already gathered, rather than a utility-specific number that doesn't exist anywhere.

## Experiment design methodology for a pilot-stage program

Flexy's realistic first-deployment scale is a single-utility pilot, most comparably sized to ComEd's own EV EMS pilot (twelve participants) or, optimistically, a low-hundreds cohort. This is far below the scale needed for standard A/B testing.

**[Fact]** Ronny Kohavi (Airbnb/Microsoft/Amazon, formerly led experimentation platforms at all three) states that detecting a realistic 5–10% lift, the range startups should target rather than 1%, requires roughly tens of thousands up to about 200,000 users for typical conversion-rate metrics. (Source: [Lenny's Podcast, "The ultimate guide to A/B testing," 2023-07-27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hEzpiDuYFoE).) A pilot-scale cohort of dozens to low hundreds is multiple orders of magnitude below this threshold at every funnel stage.

**[Fact]** Brian Tolkin (Head of Product, Opendoor; formerly Uber) describes concrete alternatives for funnel stages too low-volume for canonical A/B testing: run the power analysis first and be honest about the required runtime rather than assuming a month is enough; talk to more customers directly; use observational techniques (diff-in-diff, matched/sister-cohort comparisons); accept a lower confidence threshold (e.g. 80% instead of 95%) as a deliberate trade-off; run long-term holdouts; and, when none of that is viable, ship on intuition rather than manufacture false statistical precision. (Source: [Lenny's Podcast, "Lessons from scaling Uber and Opendoor," 2024-08-04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sRukk520Fds).)

**[Fact]** Isaac Silverman (former Head of Uber Rider Growth) describes a funnel-audit method that doesn't require running experiments to start: research the audience, walk through your own flow as each user segment would, watch non-technical users attempt it, and look at stage-by-stage data segmented by device/browser/channel to spot sharp or inconsistent drop-offs — then convert findings into hypotheses and prioritize by expected impact over cost, starting with lightweight tests. (Source: [Lenny's Newsletter, "Prioritizing conversion opportunities," 2020-05-26](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/prioritizing-conversion-opportunities).) Lenny's own heuristic in the same piece: motivation-focused changes (reminding users why to finish) tend to have the highest upside, followed by focus (removing distractions), followed by friction (making it easier) — though in practice most available opportunities end up being friction reduction, since there are simply more of them to find.

**[Fact]** For a new product with no usage history yet, Lenny's newsletter recommends determining the activation metric through interviews ("what signals that this product is solving your problem?") and a validating quantitative survey, rather than the regression-on-existing-usage-data approach that assumes a live product with real usage logs. (Source: [Lenny's Newsletter, "How to determine your activation metric," 2022-11-08](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-determine-your-activation-metric).) Flexy already has synthetic interview data (`synthetic-interviews.md`) pointing at the same activation-stage risk from a qualitative angle; the real next step this method implies is validating that signal against real ComEd customers once a pilot exists, not re-deriving it from scratch.

**[Inference]** Combining the above: Flexy's experiment design should not default to A/B testing at any funnel stage given pilot-scale volume, per Kohavi's threshold. Stage 3 (the highest-risk stage) is the best candidate for Tolkin's "talk to more customers" and qualitative-validation approaches rather than a quantitative test, at least until enrollment scales past a few hundred households. Stage 1 and Stage 2, which involve simpler binary actions (opened an invite / completed an OAuth flow) at potentially higher volume than Stage 3's harder action, are better candidates for Silverman's lightweight-test approach (message/copy variants, instruction clarity changes) even at modest scale, since detecting large, obvious differences in a simple binary outcome needs less volume than detecting a subtle lift.

## Open questions / lowest-confidence areas

- The 15–30% Stage 1 (invited→download) conversion range is this document's shakiest number — it's an Assumption bounded by one vendor's unaudited marketing claim on one side and general reasoning on the other, with no utility-specific EV/DR-program precedent found. Flag for replacement the moment any real ComEd pilot data exists.
- Whether Lenny's marketplace-category framing (Stage 3 modeled toward the low end of the activation-rate distribution) is the right analogy at all is itself an Inference, not a Fact — smart charging activation isn't literally a purchase, it's a control handoff, and no survey source measures "control handoff" activation rates specifically. Treat the resulting number as directional (low, not high) rather than a specific percentage.
- No source found anywhere (utility, vendor, or general product research) that measures a funnel structurally identical to Flexy's four-stage one. Every benchmark used here is an adjacent analog, not a direct match — this should be stated plainly in the public-facing doc rather than implied away.
