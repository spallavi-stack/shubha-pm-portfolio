Fund the Future: Personas

Synthetic. Every person below is invented. The market facts each one is built on are real and cited back to grounding-research.md, which carries the full sourcing and the confidence level for each claim. Drafted August 2026.

Seven personas across three groups. Three organisations, because the organisation is the product's user and the three differ in ways the product has to handle differently. Three donors, because the donor decides whether any of it works. One institutional visitor, who is not a user and still has to be survived.

Template per persona: identity, quote, goal, pain point, current behaviour, desired solution in their own words, tech access, jobs-to-be-done statement, the part of the product that serves them, and the researched fact the persona is built on.

Two constraints shape every organisation persona. The pilot is the Philippines, so all three are Philippine. And the target sits below the documentation floor, which after the June 2026 Council on Foundations reading means something sharper than low capacity: audited financial statements are a mandatory annual SEC filing, so an organisation without them is out of compliance with its own registration rather than merely unaccredited. Personas 1 and 2 are both in that position, which is the modal case rather than an edge one.

Group A: the organisations

1. Elena Bacani, The Compliant-Enough Organiser

Age/gender: 47, female · Location: Bantayan Island, Cebu · Role: Founder and executive director, a mangrove restoration group of nine people · Organisation age: SEC-registered since 2016 · Personal income: roughly PHP 22,000/month, paid irregularly from project money

"The register says we exist. It does not say we are honest. I have no way to show anyone the difference."

Goal: Raise PHP 400,000 for the next planting season without spending three months writing a grant application she has watched be rejected twice.

Pain point: Her General Information Sheet is filed every year because it is short and she can do it herself. Her audited financial statements are two years behind, because an audit costs money she would rather put in the ground and because the accountant who used to do it for free moved to Manila. Every platform that could carry her story treats that gap as disqualifying, and she agrees with them enough that she has stopped asking.

Current behaviour: Runs the whole thing on a Facebook page and a personal GCash number posted in the comments. Individual gifts arrive from former volunteers and from one Australian diver who visited in 2019. She has never asked a stranger for money and would not know where to put the request.

Desired solution (her words): "Show people what the government already knows about us, including the part where we are behind. If someone still wants to give after reading that, I would rather have that person than one I had to impress."

Tech access: Android phone, mobile data, no laptop at the office. Types quickly in Filipino and English. Photographs everything already, in bad light, because the work happens at low tide.

JTBD: When I need money for a planting season, I want to put what we do and what it costs in front of people who have never heard of us, so I can fund the work without a grant cycle deciding whether the mangroves get planted this year.

What serves her: Pre-onboarding vets the organisation once against the SEC register. Registered and not revoked clears the publishing floor, the two missing audited statements lower her raise cap rather than blocking her, and the page states which filings are missing in the platform's own words. The coastal and mangrove theme supplies hectares and seedlings as the counting unit so she never faces a blank metric field.

Grounding fact: The Philippine SEC runs a dedicated NPO regime where organisations file an annual General Information Sheet and audited financial statements, the free public search shows whether each was submitted, and revocation commonly follows non-filing, which is why a status of registered is evidence about the past rather than a guarantee about the present.

2. Fr. Ruben Salazar, The Man With Nothing To Count

Age/gender: 61, male · Location: Guiuan, Eastern Samar · Role: Parish priest, runs a community disaster-preparedness programme through the parish · Organisation age: SEC-registered in 2021, after Typhoon Odette · Personal income: parish stipend

"Nobody sends money for the drill. They send it for the roof, after."

Goal: Fund a year of evacuation drills, a barangay-level early warning setup and a household preparedness programme, in a place where donations arrive reliably and only after a storm.

Pain point: His work is the thing that stops the emergency, so it is invisible at exactly the moment money moves. He has no hectares, no seedlings and no tonnes. When he has tried to write a proposal he has been asked for an impact metric and has invented one, badly, because the form demanded a number and his work does not produce one on its own.

Current behaviour: Depends on a Manila-based foundation that funds him in years when it has a surplus. In 2025 it did not. He has never run a public appeal outside the parish because he does not believe his work photographs.

Desired solution (his words): "Give me the sentence. Tell me what people like me are supposed to count, and I will count it honestly. I can do the work. I cannot do the language."

Tech access: Shares a laptop in the parish office and does most things on a phone. Comfortable with Facebook and Messenger, uneasy with forms.

JTBD: When I ask for money for work that prevents a disaster rather than repairing one, I want to state it in terms a stranger can weigh, so I can compete for attention with the photographs that arrive after the storm.

What serves him: Theme 6, preparedness and capacity, which supplies its own unit because the work does not hand one over: drills run, households with a plan, people trained. This is the theme the design brief singles out as the one to build first, on the grounds that a model that works where legibility has to be invented will work everywhere else.

Grounding fact: Disaster philanthropy concentrates on the visible emergency and away from the work done beforehand. Of the $5.2bn given in 2020, 90% went to immediate needs, and in 2012 preparedness and resilience together took 14% against 46% for response and relief. That data covers foundations, donor-advised funds and corporations rather than the retail donors this product asks, which is what leaves the question open rather than closed.

3. Marisol Ocampo, The One Who Does Not Get In

Age/gender: 33, female · Location: Navotas, Metro Manila · Role: Volunteer coordinator of an unregistered shoreline waste collection group, roughly 40 people · Organisation age: four years, no legal form · Personal income: PHP 18,000/month from a separate job

"We have been doing this every Saturday for four years. Apparently that counts for nothing."

Goal: Buy a shredder and pay two collectors properly, which is about PHP 150,000.

Pain point: The group has no SEC registration, so it fails the publishing floor. It is the population the product's purpose statement points at and the one the product cannot serve, because the platform holds no money and therefore has no remedy after the fact, which leaves the gate as the only fraud control available.

Current behaviour: Raises money by selling recovered plastic and by passing a tin at community events. Has looked at registering twice and stopped at the cost and the accountant.

Desired solution (her words): "Tell me exactly what I need and how long it takes. If the answer is register first, say so on the first screen and do not waste my week."

Tech access: Android phone, confident, runs the group's whole social presence.

JTBD: When I find a platform that says it funds groups like mine, I want to learn within a minute whether it will actually take me, so I can either start the paperwork or go back to selling plastic.

What serves her: The rejection path, which the design brief records as a line rather than a screen and which this build has to make real. Reasons given, the specific missing thing named, and a route to reapply. She is in this document because a persona the product turns away is a design requirement, and because her existence is the honest limit of the purpose statement.

Grounding fact: 64,087 NPOs were registered with the SEC across all classifications as of December 2020, and no aggregate has been published since. The unregistered population is not measured at all, which means the size of the group this product excludes is unknown rather than small.

Group B: the donors

4. Anders Holm, The Checker

Age/gender: 52, male · Location: Utrecht, Netherlands · Occupation: Civil engineer, water management · Household: Married, two teenagers · Income: roughly EUR 88,000

"I will read all of it. If the numbers do not add up I will not give, and I will not tell you why."

Goal: Give roughly EUR 1,200 a year to climate work and know where each part of it went.

Pain point: He already gives to two large environmental organisations and cannot tell what his money did in either. He wants to fund something specific and small, and every small thing he finds is unverifiable, which for him is the same as untrustworthy.

Current behaviour: Reads annual reports. Checked one organisation's registration in a national register before giving. Abandoned two donation pages in the past year because the money's use was described in adjectives.

Desired solution (his words): "Show me the line items and show me what somebody other than the organisation has confirmed. I can tell the difference between a claim and a check, and most pages are hoping I cannot."

Tech access: High. Will open a register in a second tab and verify a number himself.

JTBD: When I decide where this year's climate giving goes, I want to see what an independent party has confirmed about an organisation separately from what the organisation says about itself, so I can fund small work without taking it on faith.

What serves him: The "what was checked" section, and the rule that facts the platform verified carry a different visual treatment from things the organisation stated, applied without exception so the distinction is learnable within one page. The costed line-by-line budget is the other half.

Grounding fact: Asked what would encourage them to give more, donors worldwide put "be transparent around how the charity is run" first at 47%, ahead of "clearly explain how they are making change" at 40% and well ahead of making donation easier at 23%. Climate donors are separately the highest-due-diligence segment in the IRS donor database, which is the weakest sourcing behind any load-bearing claim in this document and is on the handover's worklist for a primary upgrade. Both figures are stated preference rather than observed behaviour, which is why hypothesis 2 in the product brief exists to test it.

5. Priya Nair-Whitcombe, The Standing Order

Age/gender: 38, female · Location: Bristol, United Kingdom · Occupation: NHS paediatric physiotherapist · Household: Partner, one child · Income: roughly GBP 46,000

"I do not want to make this decision twelve times a year. I want to make it once."

Goal: Give GBP 20 a month to something that is still there in three years.

Pain point: She has given one-off amounts to disaster appeals and felt nothing afterwards, because the appeal ended and no one told her what happened. She would rather commit smaller and longer, and most small organisations only offer a one-time button.

Current behaviour: Three monthly direct debits, all to UK charities, all set up years ago and never reviewed. Gift Aid ticked on all three.

Desired solution (her words): "Let me set it monthly at the same moment I decide, and then actually tell me what happened. If you only email me when you want more money I will cancel."

Tech access: Phone, mostly. Gives in the evening, from a link a friend sent.

JTBD: When I find work worth supporting, I want to commit monthly in the same action rather than in a second decision later, so my giving continues without me having to remember it.

What serves her: Recurring giving shipping in the MVP, with one-off and monthly presented at the same decision point rather than the monthly option arriving as an upsell. The reporting commitment section, which states what will be reported and when before any money moves, is what earns the second year.

Grounding fact: In the environmental cut of the M+R 2026 benchmarks, monthly giving is 32% of online revenue against 27% across all causes, retention runs 53% against 48%, and new-donor retention 28% against 24%. Average one-time gift is lower, $93 against $139. The environmental donor gives less per gift and stays longer, which is the strongest single argument in the research for shipping recurring giving rather than deferring it, and it matters doubly under donor tips, where platform revenue tracks donor volume rather than gift size.

What does not serve her: UK Gift Aid requires an HMRC-recognised charity, so she gets no relief on this gift. She is the persona most likely to notice.

6. Tomas Reyes-Mbeki, The Browser

Age/gender: 24, male · Location: Toronto, Canada · Occupation: Junior data analyst · Household: Shares a flat · Income: roughly CAD 62,000

"I know I am supposed to care. I do care. I just have never known where to put it."

Goal: Give something, this evening, to climate work that is real, and stop feeling that caring about it produces nothing.

Pain point: His concern is high and his sense that anything he does matters is low, which is the combination the research says produces paralysis rather than action. He has no organisation in mind and no link from a friend. He arrives at the platform itself, which is the arrival the Matchmaker exists for.

Current behaviour: Has signed petitions, attended one march, given money once, to a crowdfunder for a friend's surgery. Has typed "climate charity" into a search engine twice and closed the tab both times.

Desired solution (his words): "Give me a small number of real options and let me pick on something I actually understand, like what kind of work it is and how much is still needed. Do not rank them for me and do not tell me which one is best."

Tech access: High, phone-first, low patience for a page that takes more than a moment to make sense.

JTBD: When climate news leaves me feeling useless, I want to find a specific bounded piece of work I can fund tonight, so the concern turns into something that happened instead of something I felt.

What serves him: The Matchmaker, which is why it survived a locked decision that ruled it out. He is the donor who never receives a shared link. The constraint the handover records applies directly to him: whatever orders that list has to be a fact he chose, such as the kind of work or the amount still needed, rather than a platform judgment of quality, because a ranking is the badge that locked decision 6 rules out.

Grounding fact: Climate anxiety converts into action for some people and into paralysis for others, with self-efficacy as the mediating variable, and despair suppresses the giving that worry might otherwise produce. What moves money is a specific, credible, bounded thing to do at the moment the concern is felt. Among 16 to 25 year olds across ten countries, 59% are very or extremely worried and 75% say the future is frightening.

Group C: the visitor

7. Dr Grace Otieno, The Programme Officer

Age/gender: 44, female · Location: Nairobi, Kenya · Role: Programme officer, adaptation portfolio, a mid-sized international foundation · Portfolio: roughly $6m a year in grants between $25,000 and $300,000

"I did not come here to give. I came here because someone sent me a link and I want to know whether this organisation is worth forty minutes."

Goal: Decide quickly whether an organisation she has never heard of is worth putting into her own pipeline.

Pain point: Her sourcing problem is the mirror of the organisation's funding problem. She is looking for exactly the groups that are too small to appear anywhere she looks, and the ones she does find arrive with no verifiable record.

Current behaviour: Sources through existing grantees, conferences and intermediaries. Has funded one organisation in three years that came to her cold.

Desired solution (her words): "I need the registration number, the filing record and the name of the person who is accountable for the money. If the page has those I can start. If it has a mission statement and a photograph I am done in ten seconds."

Tech access: High, and she will verify the SEC number herself.

JTBD: When a link reaches me from outside my network, I want the organisation's registry identity and its accountable person available immediately, so I can decide whether to spend real diligence time on it.

What serves her: Nothing is built for her, and the product surface decision holds: no funder discovery surface, no directory, no grantmaker matching. She arrives through a link like any other visitor and the page has to survive her anyway. The SEC number, the filing record and the named accountable person are the three things that let her act, and all three are already on the page for the donor's sake.

Grounding fact: In humanitarian funding, the best-tracked analog, $1.7bn reached local and national actors directly in 2023, 4.5% of trackable funding, against a 25% target set in 2016 that has never been met. The sourcing gap she describes is documented at sector scale, which is why she is worth designing to survive even though the product sells her nothing.

What these personas commit the product to

Seven personas produce five requirements that are not negotiable in the build.

  1. The gap is priced by the platform, never interpreted by the donor. Elena publishes with two missing audited statements and a lower cap. Anders reads what is missing in the platform's words. Neither of them is asked to make the other's judgment.
  2. The theme supplies the unit. Ruben is the reason theme 6 exists and the reason it gets built first.
  3. Rejection is a designed path. Marisol gets a reason, a named missing thing and a route back.
  4. Monthly and one-off sit at the same decision point. Priya decides once.
  5. The Matchmaker orders on a fact the donor chose. Tomas needs to arrive without a link. Ordering on anything the platform judges reintroduces the badge.

What these personas do not establish

The donor personas rest on benchmark data drawn overwhelmingly from US organisations with established digital programmes, and on stated preference rather than observed behaviour. The single deepest open assumption in the project is untouched by any of them: whether individual donors will fund adaptation specifically, as distinct from disaster relief. Tomas and Anders are constructed as though they will. Ruben's grounding fact is the evidence that they might not. The concept test in the product brief's success-metrics section is what settles it, and these personas are its input rather than its answer.
