# Fund the Future: Pilot Market Selection

## Status: rewritten August 2026

This document has been rewritten twice. The first version named Kenya, the Philippines and India, chosen by association rather than against criteria. The second redid the selection properly against six ranked criteria and produced a three-market pilot of the Philippines, Nigeria and Honduras.

**This version makes two changes.**

**One: the pilot is a single market.** Researching payout rails and verification to the depth this product requires produced three partial answers rather than one complete one. Expansion is a post-MVP question.

**Two: criterion 4, diaspora size and remittance corridor strength, has been deleted, and every market's score has been re-derived without it.** The reasoning behind that criterion was that a builder-plus-link product with no discovery surface travels only as far as an organisation's own network carries it, which made a large higher-income diaspora look like the most plausible donor pool. That reasoning conflated two different behaviours. Remittance is money sent to one's own family under obligation. Donating to a climate organisation is charitable giving to strangers. Proximity to a country is not proximity to a cause, and no evidence in this research connects the two. A market selected for remittance strength was selected on the wrong evidence.

**The donor for this product is a climate-motivated retail donor**, meaning someone who already gives to environmental causes and would give to adaptation work if it were legible, specific and trustworthy. Nothing about that donor makes any particular recipient country more or less suitable, so the surviving criteria are all about the recipient side.

Every claim below is tagged **Fact** (cited), **Inference** (reasoned from stated facts), or **Assumption** (unverified). Claims sourced only from search snippets rather than the primary document are marked as such and carry a verification task.

## Method

**Stage 1: hard filters.** Pass or fail. A country failing any filter is out regardless of how well it scores elsewhere, because each filter breaks the product's core operation rather than degrading it.

**Stage 2: five ranked criteria**, applied to the survivors. Three were set by the user, two were added for product-specific reasons.

| # | Criterion | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Impact of the climate crisis on the country | User |
| 2 | Size and documentability of the nonprofit sector, measured by total registered NPO count, with an explicit flag where the registration regime likely hides a large informal sector | User, restated August 2026 |
| 3 | Population in or near poverty, measured both as a rate and as an absolute count, reported separately | User |
| 4 | Whether a public NGO registry exists that can actually be queried | Added |
| 5 | Whether an established, documented payout rail already reaches organisations in the country | Added |

**Why criteria 4 and 5 were added, and why those reasons survive.** Verification is the product's stated differentiator, so a market with no checkable register is a poor place to pilot a trust product. Cross-border money movement is the product's defining technical constraint, so payout rails work two ways: **pass or fail on whether any documented rail reaches the country at all**, and **ranked on how established that rail is**, measured by number of channels, breadth of receiving institutions, and whether organisations without a traditional bank account can be paid.

**[Inference]** With diaspora removed, criteria 4 and 5 carry more weight than before, because they are the two that map directly onto the product's two hard problems. That is a defensible reweighting rather than a convenient one: the product's difficulty is proving an organisation is real and getting money to it, and these are the two criteria that measure exactly that.

**Criterion 2 was restated in August 2026 because its original form was never satisfiable.** It previously asked for the number of registered climate or adaptation NGOs. That figure was not obtainable for any candidate market in any of three research passes, and Kenya, now eliminated, is the only country where a government-published sector breakdown exists at all. A scoring criterion that scores nobody is decorative, which is the problem this document exists to fix.

The criterion now measures **total registered NPO count**, which is available and already collected for the leading candidates. **[Inference]** This preserves what the criterion was actually asking, meaning whether a real pool of organisations exists and whether the state keeps track of them, while making it answerable. It also strengthens the Philippines, which holds the largest verified count of any candidate, so the restatement is declared here rather than left to look like a convenient adjustment made after the selection.

**The informal-sector flag survives the restatement and matters more under it.** Registered counts systematically undercount this product's target user, because the product exists for organisations too small or too informal to appear on a register at all.

## Stage 1: hard filters

### Filter A: foreign donations must be legally receivable by small organisations
An approval gate that most small organisations cannot clear makes the product unusable for its target user, regardless of need.

**Eliminated:** India (Foreign Contribution Regulation Act, with amendments under consideration that would expand government authority further), Bangladesh (Foreign Donations Regulation Act approval gate), Vietnam (Decree 80), Pakistan, Uganda (Protection of Sovereignty Bill, 2026), Zimbabwe, China, Egypt, Nicaragua, El Salvador (Foreign Agents Law, May 2025). (**Fact** for India, Uganda and El Salvador, per [ICNL](https://www.icnl.org/our-work/cross-border-funding) and [WOLA](https://www.wola.org/analysis/curtailing-civic-space-tightening-restrictions-on-civil-society-in-the-americas/); **Inference** for the remainder, from the same restriction pattern, and each needs verification before any of them is reconsidered.)

**Nepal** requires Social Welfare Council approval for foreign funding, which is the same category of gate the user asked to avoid. (**Assumption**, not verified. Worth verifying if the shortlist is reopened.) Note that Nepal was previously described here as a painful loss because of its remittance-to-GDP ratio. That reasoning is void. It remains a reasonable candidate on climate exposure alone.

**Ghana: unresolved.** Ghanaian NGOs register under the Companies Act, whose provisions facilitate NGO formation, and no specific foreign-funding restriction was located. But no source positively confirms that foreign donations are receivable without an approval gate. (**Assumption**, per [ICNL sub-Saharan Africa survey](https://www.icnl.org/wp-content/uploads/global-ngo-law_trends3-3.pdf), which documents a regional trend toward prior-approval requirements without resolving Ghana's current position.) **Ghana cannot be selected until this is verified**, and this is a genuine gap rather than a reason to dismiss it.

### Filter B: a documented payout rail must reach the country
**Eliminated:** Somalia, Afghanistan, Syria, Yemen, South Sudan.

All markets reaching stage 2 were verified against primary provider documentation rather than assumed.

### Filter C: no comprehensive sanctions, and no conflict state that makes payouts impossible
**Eliminated:** Myanmar, Sudan, Democratic Republic of Congo (eastern provinces), Haiti, Venezuela.

**Mozambique is now eliminated here.** It was previously set aside for a weak diaspora, which is no longer a reason for anything, so it was re-examined against the hard filters directly. The Cabo Delgado insurgency has displaced close to a million people, with roughly 660,000 internally displaced across Cabo Delgado, Niassa and Nampula and a 2026 humanitarian response plan requiring US$348m across the three northern provinces. (**Fact**, per [OCHA](https://www.unocha.org/publications/report/mozambique/mozambique-2026-humanitarian-response-conflict-31-january-2026) and [ECHO](https://civil-protection-humanitarian-aid.ec.europa.eu/where/africa/mozambique_en).) It also has an unresolved filter A exposure: a draft NGO law introduced in 2022, framed as anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing measures, imposes excessive formation requirements, grants officials overbroad discretion over authorisation, allows arbitrary dissolution, and imposes civil liability on officers and members. (**Fact** on the draft's content, per [American Bar Association analysis](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/human_rights/reports/mozambique-ngo-law-analysis/). **Assumption:** whether it has been enacted was not established.) Government forces have also been accused of harassment and intimidation of civil society organisations in the insurgency response.

**[Inference]** Mozambique fails filter C on the same basis as eastern DRC, and would need filter A resolved even if it did not. Its elimination does not depend on the deleted criterion, which is the point of re-examining it.

### FATF grey-listing
Not a hard filter, because it does not make the product illegal. Scored as a significant negative, because it drives correspondent-bank de-risking and heightened scrutiny of nonprofit payment flows, which lands directly on the product's core operation of moving donor money to small nonprofits.

As of 19 June 2026, 22 jurisdictions are under increased monitoring, including **Kenya** and **Nepal**. (**Fact**, secondary-sourced via [ComplyAdvantage](https://complyadvantage.com/insights/fatf-plenary-june-2026-outcomes/); the FATF's own page returned 403 to automated fetching, so this should be re-verified manually against [FATF's June 2026 statement](https://www.fatf-gafi.org/en/publications/High-risk-and-other-monitored-jurisdictions/increased-monitoring-june-2026.html).)

The Philippines was removed in February 2025, and Nigeria and Mozambique in October 2025. (**Fact**, per [The Diplomat](https://thediplomat.com/2025/02/philippines-removed-from-watchdogs-money-laundering-grey-list/) and [Aluko & Oyebode](https://www.aluko-oyebode.com/insights/nigeria-fatf-grey-list-removal-2025/).)

## Selection: the Philippines

**The Philippines is selected on criteria 4 and 5, supported by criterion 1.** No part of this rests on diaspora, remittance flows, or population living abroad.

- **Registry, criterion 4.** The SEC register of non-stock corporations operates a dedicated NPO regime under SEC Memorandum Circular 25 series of 2019. Organisations file annual General Information Sheets and audited financial statements. (**Fact**, per [Council on Foundations](https://cof.org/sites/default/files/2025-08/Nonprofit-Law-in-Philippines.pdf).) **[Inference]** This is the decisive advantage. Verification is the product's stated pillar, and the Philippines is the only candidate whose register carries a continuing filing obligation rather than a one-off record of existence. A register that shows an organisation filed accounts last year proves something a register of names cannot.
- **Payout rail, criterion 5.** dLocal documents both bank transfers and real-time wallet transfers into the Philippines, in PHP and USD, covering GCash, PayMaya, Coins, GrabPay, Starpay, TayoCash and PalawanPay, with wallet transfers capped at PHP 50,000 per transaction. (**Fact**, primary source, [dLocal Philippines payouts documentation](https://docs.dlocal.com/docs/philippines-payouts-v3).) Xendit provides a like-for-like alternative across 94+ banks and 13 e-wallets. **[Inference]** The wallet channel matters more than the bank channel, because an organisation with no bank account can still be paid, and that organisation is precisely the target user. Two like-for-like providers also removes the single-provider concentration risk that Honduras carries.
- **Climate impact, criterion 1.** Among the ten most affected countries in the Climate Risk Index long-term ranking, one of five lower-middle-income countries in that top ten, and second on composite climate-risk scoring at 83.1 behind only Bangladesh. (**Fact**, per [Germanwatch CRI 2025](https://www.germanwatch.org/sites/default/files/2025-02/Climate%20Risk%20Index%202025.pdf); the 83.1 composite figure is secondary-sourced and needs a primary check.)
- **Registered NPOs, criterion 2.** 64,087 NPOs registered across classifications as of 31 December 2020, the largest verified count of any candidate. (**Fact**, search-snippet sourced to SEC data; re-verify against the [AMLC NPO Risk Assessment](http://www.amlc.gov.ph/images/PDFs/NPO%20Risk%20Assessment.pdf) and obtain a current figure.) A climate or environment subset was not found. Under the restated criterion this is no longer a gap in the scoring, though it remains useful for sizing the addressable population.
- **Poverty, criterion 3.** 15.5% of the population, 17.54 million people, in 2023, down from 19.99 million in 2021. Substantial in absolute terms and moderate as a rate, making it the weakest of the candidates on this criterion. (**Fact**, verified, per [PSA 2023 Full Year Official Poverty Statistics](https://psa.gov.ph/sites/default/files/phdsd/2023%20FY%20Official%20Poverty%20Statistics%20Publication_15August2024.pdf).)
- **Foreign donations.** Permitted. Receiving foreign grants triggers additional SEC, BIR and AMLA reporting rather than an approval gate. (**Fact**, same Council on Foundations source.)
- **Supporting evidence found after selection.** Asia and Oceania received under 10% of foundation adaptation funding between 2021 and 2024 while holding more than half the world's population, and disaster risk management and infrastructure were the least funded sectors over that period. (**Fact**, [ClimateWorks](https://www.climateworks.org/press-release/funding-for-climate-adaptation-and-resilience-hits-record-of-870-million/), see `grounding-research.md` section 13.) **[Inference]** The pilot market sits in the region most exposed by population and least served by adaptation philanthropy, and the work most common among target organisations falls in the least-funded sectors.

**The honest weakness.** The Philippines is the weakest of the candidates on poverty rate and does not lead on climate impact. It is selected because it is strongest on the two criteria that map to the product's two hard problems, which is a deliberate weighting stated openly rather than an overall score.

## Why not the others

### Honduras
Strongest climate-impact signal on the entire longlist: third most affected country in the world in both editions of the Climate Risk Index, the only candidate in the top three of both. (**Fact**, per [Germanwatch CRI 2026](https://www.germanwatch.org/en/cri) and [CRI 2025](https://www.germanwatch.org/sites/default/files/2025-02/Climate%20Risk%20Index%202025.pdf).) Its registry, URSAC, operates under the 2011 NGO-D Law and its 2013 Regulations, which established a closed list of registration requirements specifically to limit official discretion, and Honduras has a Special Law for the Promotion of Non-Governmental Development Organizations explicitly covering environmental protection. (**Fact**, per [ICNL Honduras](https://www.icnl.org/resources/civic-freedom-monitor/honduras).)

**Ruled out on criterion 5.** dLocal documents bank transfers into 16 Honduran banks in HNL and USD, with beneficiaries required to supply a DNI or RTN. There is no wallet channel. (**Fact**, primary source, [dLocal Honduras payouts documentation](https://docs.dlocal.com/docs/honduras-payouts-v3).) The only verified alternative, Nium, pays a Visa card in HNL or wires over SWIFT, so it reaches a different destination type rather than substituting. (**Fact**, [Nium Honduras playbook](https://playbook.nium.com/country/honduras).) **[Inference]** An organisation needs a formal bank account to be paid at all, which is the exact barrier the product exists to lower, and the market is single-rail for local-currency payout.

**Second reason.** The government has restricted foreign donations administratively through the tax authority during the COVID-19 pandemic and in response to natural disasters, which is precisely when this product would see peak demand. (**Fact**, verified, per ICNL Honduras.) A general election in November 2025 carried associated pressure on civil society transparency. (**Fact**, per [CEPR](https://cepr.net/publications/honduras-social-economic-indicators-ahead-of-2025-general-election/).)

**[Inference]** In a three-market pilot Honduras was worth carrying, because its weaknesses forced honesty. As the sole pilot market it puts the payout rail, which is the product's defining technical constraint, in its weakest available configuration.

### Nigeria
Holds the largest absolute number of people in extreme poverty of any country, roughly 12% of the global total, and is projected with DRC to account for a quarter of global extreme poverty by 2030. (**Fact**, search-snippet sourced to World Bank reporting; re-verify against the [Nigeria Poverty and Equity Brief](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099253204222517873/txt/IDU-3e010784-3371-4dcb-b923-112e98052ed9.txt).) Foreign funding is unrestricted, with repeated legislative proposals over nearly two decades never enacted. (**Fact**, verified, per [ICNL Nigeria](https://www.icnl.org/resources/civic-freedom-monitor/nigeria).) Its payout rail is strong and well suited to the target user: dLocal documents transfers into 100+ institutions in NGN and USD, extending past traditional banks to microfinance banks and fintech providers including Moniepoint, PalmPay and OPay, with Flutterwave as a like-for-like alternative. (**Fact**, primary source, [dLocal Nigeria payouts documentation](https://docs.dlocal.com/docs/nigeria-payouts-v3).) **[Inference]** A small informal community group is more likely to hold an OPay or Moniepoint account than a traditional bank account, so the rail reaches the target user.

**Ruled out on criteria 1 and 4.** Nigeria does not appear near the top of the Climate Risk Index or ND-GAIN vulnerability rankings, despite serious exposure through flooding, Lake Chad desiccation and desertification. (**Inference**, from its absence from those rankings.) Its registry, the Corporate Affairs Commission's Incorporated Trustees regime under Part F of the Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020, sections 825 to 829, offers a public name search, and whether full organisational records are publicly queryable was never established. (**Fact** on the legal framework, **open question** on registry depth, per [Council on Foundations](https://cof.org/country-notes/nonprofit-law-nigeria).)

**[Inference]** For an adaptation product, leading on poverty while ranking low on climate impact is the wrong pair of scores. Nigeria is the strongest candidate if the product is ever reframed around domestic giving, since it ranked first in the world by share of income donated in the 2025 CAF World Giving Report. That is an expansion note, not a reason to select it now.

### Kenya
Best-documented market on the longlist and the only one with a government-published NGO count and sector breakdown: 12,643 registered as of 30 June 2023, 10,279 active, with Environment sixth largest by expenditure at KES 6.18bn, 5.64% of sector spending, Water and Sanitation at 5.07% and Agriculture at 4.39%. (**Fact**, primary source, [Annual NGO Sector Report 2022/2023](https://www.pbora.go.ke/sites/default/files/2025-09/Annual%20NGOS%20Sector%20Report%202022-2023.pdf).) Foreign funding is legally unrestricted. (**Fact**, per [ICNL Kenya](https://www.icnl.org/resources/civic-freedom-monitor/kenya).) Its payout rail is the most established in the world: dLocal documents bank transfers plus real-time wallet transfers to M-Pesa, Airtel Money and Equitel, addressed by phone number. (**Fact**, primary source, [dLocal Kenya payouts documentation](https://docs.dlocal.com/docs/kenya-payouts-v3).)

**Ruled out on FATF exposure.** Kenya is on the grey list as of June 2026, and grey-listing drives correspondent-bank de-risking and heightened scrutiny of nonprofit payment flows, which is the exact operation a payout rail performs. Separately, in 2024 the government accused 16 civil society organisations of misusing foreign funding and referred their files to the Directorate of Criminal Investigations, so foreign-funded civil society is politically exposed despite being legally unrestricted. (**Fact**, per ICNL Kenya.)

**[Inference]** On criteria 4 and 5 alone Kenya beats the Philippines. Removing the diaspora criterion strengthens Kenya's case rather than weakening it, and this should be said plainly. **Override condition:** Kenya is the correct selection if grey-list exposure is judged manageable and registry data quality plus payout maturity are weighted above everything else. It is the better-documented market and, given the political exposure of foreign-funded civil society there, the riskier one for a product whose entire operation is receiving foreign donations.

### Ghana
Payout rail matches the Philippines' shape: bank transfers plus real-time mobile money to MTN, Vodafone, AirtelTigo and G-Money, in GHS and USD, with a Ghana Card required for certain transaction types. (**Fact**, primary source, [dLocal Ghana payouts documentation](https://docs.dlocal.com/docs/ghana-payouts-v3).) Clean on FATF.

**Not selectable yet.** Filter A is unresolved, as set out above. **[Inference]** Ghana was previously dismissed partly for a small diaspora, which is no longer a reason. With that removed, it is a legitimate candidate blocked only by an unverified hard filter, and verifying Ghana's foreign-funding position is now a live research task rather than a closed question. Its climate-impact ranking and poverty rate are both materially below the leading candidates, so verification would likely still leave it behind the Philippines on criterion 1.

### Guatemala
Bank transfers only, reaching 19 financial institutions against Honduras' 16, with a 13-digit CUI required. No wallet channel. (**Fact**, primary source, [dLocal Guatemala payouts documentation](https://docs.dlocal.com/docs/guatemala-payouts-v3).) Ranked 14th among the twenty most affected countries for 1998 to 2017. (**Fact**, search-snippet sourced, per [CGIAR CCAFS](https://ccafs.cgiar.org/regions/latin-america/guatemala).) No restrictions on domestic CSOs receiving foreign funding (**Fact**, per [ICNL Guatemala](https://www.icnl.org/resources/civic-freedom-monitor/guatemala)), though its 2020 NGO Law, Decree 04-2020, complicates registration and a reform bill has been pending since 2024. Carries Honduras' payout weakness with a weaker climate-impact ranking.

### Maldives
Permissive foreign-funding regime and existential climate exposure, but a population of roughly half a million cannot compete on criterion 2 or criterion 3 against any other candidate. It was selected in an earlier pass on qualitative argument rather than against the criteria.

### Also considered
Madagascar, Malawi, Tanzania, Zambia, Senegal, Vanuatu, Solomon Islands, Fiji, Indonesia, Colombia and Peru were on the longlist and did not reach the shortlist on the combined criteria.

## The finding that matters most, and it is not about country selection

Kenya's sector report contains a number that should shape the product brief. In FY 2022/23, Kenyan NGOs received KES 196bn. **Individual donors accounted for 2.3% of it, and that source shrank 10% year on year.** The sector runs on affiliates (27.6%), other NGOs, foundations and trusts (25.8%), and foreign government agencies (21.2%). (**Fact**, primary source, same report.)

Fund the Future is a retail-giving product aimed at a sector where retail giving is around 2% of income and falling. Sections 10 and 13 of `grounding-research.md` partially explain why: cross-border philanthropy is roughly 3% of global giving, and adaptation is under 10% of climate finance within that. What remains unresolved is whether individual donors will fund adaptation work specifically.

A second figure from the same report bears directly on the verification pillar: only 2,829 of 10,279 active NGOs filed annual reports that year. Registration status alone proves less about an organisation than it appears to, even in the best-documented market examined. (**Fact**, same report.) **[Inference]** This is also the strongest argument for the Philippines' annual filing obligation being the right thing to build verification on, and the strongest caution against assuming a filing obligation means filings happen.
