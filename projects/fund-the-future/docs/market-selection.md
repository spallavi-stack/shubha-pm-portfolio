# Fund the Future: Pilot Market Selection

## Why this document exists

The first pass at choosing pilot markets named Kenya, the Philippines, and India, and then swapped India for Maldives. Kenya and the Philippines were chosen by association rather than by evidence: they are the countries that come up most often in climate-finance conversation, which is a bias, not a criterion. This document redoes the selection against stated criteria, applied to a longlist, with the data sourced.

Two of the original three do not survive the exercise. That is the point of running it.

Every claim below is tagged **Fact** (cited), **Inference** (reasoned from stated facts), or **Assumption** (unverified). Claims sourced only from search snippets rather than the primary document are marked as such and carry a verification task.

## Method

**Stage 1: hard filters.** Pass or fail. A country failing any filter is out regardless of how well it scores elsewhere, because each filter breaks the product's core operation rather than degrading it.

**Stage 2: ranked criteria.** Applied to the survivors. Five criteria, three set by the user and two added:

1. Impact of the climate crisis on the country
2. Number of registered NGOs working on climate mitigation or adaptation, with an explicit flag where the registration regime likely hides a large informal sector
3. Population in or near poverty, measured both as a rate and as an absolute count, reported separately
4. Diaspora size and remittance corridor strength
5. Whether a public NGO registry exists that can actually be queried
6. Whether an established, documented payout rail already reaches organisations in the country

Criteria 4, 5 and 6 were added for product-specific reasons. The platform is a builder plus a shareable link with no discovery surface, so a campaign travels exactly as far as the organisation's own network carries it, which makes a large higher-income diaspora the most plausible donor pool. Verification is the product's stated differentiator, so a market with no checkable register is a poor place to pilot a trust product.

Criterion 6 was added after the first research pass, and it corrects a weakness in this document's own method. "A working payout rail must exist" was originally a hard filter only, used to eliminate obvious cases like Somalia and Yemen, and then assumed to hold for the survivors without being demonstrated for any of them. That is backwards for a product whose defining technical constraint is cross-border money movement. Payout rails now work two ways: **pass or fail on whether any documented rail reaches the country at all**, and **ranked on how established that rail is**, measured by number of channels, breadth of receiving institutions, and whether organisations without a traditional bank account can be paid.

## Stage 1: hard filters

### Filter A: foreign donations must be legally receivable by small organisations
An approval gate that most small organisations cannot clear makes the product unusable for its target user, regardless of need.

**Eliminated:** India (Foreign Contribution Regulation Act, with amendments under consideration that would expand government authority further), Bangladesh (Foreign Donations Regulation Act approval gate), Vietnam (Decree 80), Pakistan, Uganda (Protection of Sovereignty Bill, 2026), Zimbabwe, China, Egypt, Nicaragua, El Salvador (Foreign Agents Law, May 2025). (**Fact** for India, Uganda, and El Salvador, per [ICNL](https://www.icnl.org/our-work/cross-border-funding) and [WOLA](https://www.wola.org/analysis/curtailing-civic-space-tightening-restrictions-on-civil-society-in-the-americas/); **Inference** for the remainder, from the same restriction pattern, and each needs verification before any of them is reconsidered.)

**Nepal is the painful elimination here.** It has one of the world's highest remittance-to-GDP ratios and severe Himalayan climate exposure, which would have made it a strong candidate. Foreign funding requires Social Welfare Council approval, which is the same category of gate the user asked to avoid. (**Assumption**, not verified this pass. Worth verifying if the shortlist is ever reopened, because Nepal scores well on everything else.)

### Filter B: a documented payout rail must reach the country
**Eliminated:** Somalia, Afghanistan, Syria, Yemen, South Sudan.

All three selected markets were subsequently verified against primary provider documentation rather than assumed. See criterion 6 scoring below.

### Filter C: no comprehensive sanctions, and no conflict state that makes payouts impossible
**Eliminated:** Myanmar, Sudan, Democratic Republic of Congo (eastern provinces), Haiti, Venezuela.

This filter is expensive. Myanmar ranks second most affected in the Climate Risk Index 2026 long-term ranking (**Fact**, per [Germanwatch](https://www.germanwatch.org/en/cri)), and DRC holds roughly 11.7% of the world's extreme poor (**Fact**, search-snippet sourced, per World Bank reporting). Both would rank near the top on the user's criteria and neither can be served.

### A note on FATF grey-listing
Grey-listing is not a hard filter, because it does not make the product illegal. It is scored as a significant negative because it drives correspondent-bank de-risking and heightened scrutiny of nonprofit payment flows, which lands directly on this product's core operation of moving donor money to small nonprofits.

As of 19 June 2026, 22 jurisdictions are under increased monitoring: Angola, Bolivia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Bulgaria, Cameroon, Côte d'Ivoire, DR Congo, Haiti, Iraq, **Kenya**, Kuwait, Laos, Lebanon, Monaco, **Nepal**, Papua New Guinea, South Sudan, Syria, Venezuela, Vietnam, Virgin Islands (UK), and Yemen. (**Fact**, secondary-sourced via [ComplyAdvantage](https://complyadvantage.com/insights/fatf-plenary-june-2026-outcomes/) and [an AML compliance summary](https://amluae.com/fatf-grey-list-countries-update-19th-june-2026/); the FATF's own page returned 403 to automated fetching, so this list should be re-verified manually against [FATF's June 2026 statement](https://www.fatf-gafi.org/en/publications/High-risk-and-other-monitored-jurisdictions/increased-monitoring-june-2026.html).)

The Philippines was removed in February 2025, and Nigeria and Mozambique in October 2025. (**Fact**, per [The Diplomat](https://thediplomat.com/2025/02/philippines-removed-from-watchdogs-money-laundering-grey-list/) and [Aluko & Oyebode](https://www.aluko-oyebode.com/insights/nigeria-fatf-grey-list-removal-2025/).)

## Stage 2: the recommended three

### 1. Philippines
Strongest candidate on the combined criteria, and the only country that scores well on all five.

- **Climate impact:** Among the ten most affected countries in the Climate Risk Index long-term ranking, and one of five lower-middle-income countries in that top ten. Also ranks second on composite climate-risk scoring at 83.1, behind only Bangladesh. (**Fact**, per [Germanwatch CRI 2025](https://www.germanwatch.org/sites/default/files/2025-02/Climate%20Risk%20Index%202025.pdf); the 83.1 composite figure is secondary-sourced and needs a primary check.)
- **Registered NGOs:** 64,087 NPOs registered across classifications as of 31 December 2020, the largest verified count of any candidate. (**Fact**, search-snippet sourced to SEC data; re-verify against the [AMLC NPO Risk Assessment](http://www.amlc.gov.ph/images/PDFs/NPO%20Risk%20Assessment.pdf) and obtain a current figure.) A climate or environment subset was not found and remains an open research task.
- **Diaspora:** Third largest remittance recipient in the world in 2024, behind India and Mexico. Best diaspora reach of any candidate by a wide margin. (**Fact**, per [IOM World Migration Report](https://worldmigrationreport.iom.int/what-we-do/world-migration-report-2026/chapter-2/international-remittances).)
- **Registry:** SEC register of non-stock corporations, with a dedicated NPO regime under SEC Memorandum Circular 25 series of 2019. Organisations file annual General Information Sheets and audited financial statements, which gives verification more to check than existence alone. (**Fact**, per [Council on Foundations](https://cof.org/sites/default/files/2025-08/Nonprofit-Law-in-Philippines.pdf).)
- **Foreign donations:** Permitted. Receiving foreign grants triggers additional SEC, BIR, and AMLA reporting rather than an approval gate. (**Fact**, same source.)
- **FATF:** Clean since February 2025.
- **Payout rail (criterion 6): strongest of the three.** dLocal documents both bank transfers and real-time wallet transfers into the Philippines, in PHP and USD. Supported wallets include GCash, PayMaya, Coins, GrabPay, Starpay, TayoCash, and PalawanPay. Wallet transfers are capped at PHP 50,000 per transaction. B2C, B2B, and P2P flows are all supported. (**Fact**, primary source, [dLocal Philippines payouts documentation](https://docs.dlocal.com/docs/philippines-payouts-v3).) The wallet channel matters more than the bank channel here, because an organisation with no bank account can still be paid.
- **Poverty:** 15.5% of the population, or 17.54 million Filipinos, in 2023, down from 19.99 million in 2021. Substantial in absolute terms and moderate as a rate, which makes it the weakest of the three on criterion 3. (**Fact**, verified, per [PSA 2023 Full Year Official Poverty Statistics](https://psa.gov.ph/sites/default/files/phdsd/2023%20FY%20Official%20Poverty%20Statistics%20Publication_15August2024.pdf).)

### 2. Nigeria
Wins criterion 3 outright and brings the second-strongest diaspora.

- **Poverty:** Holds the largest absolute number of people in extreme poverty of any country in the world, roughly 12% of the global total, and is projected with DRC to account for a quarter of global extreme poverty by 2030. (**Fact**, search-snippet sourced to World Bank reporting; re-verify against the [Nigeria Poverty and Equity Brief](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099253204222517873/txt/IDU-3e010784-3371-4dcb-b923-112e98052ed9.txt).)
- **Diaspora:** Received $20.93bn in remittances in 2024, up 8.9% year on year, and $21.8bn in 2025. Nigeria's 2023 inflow of $19.5bn was 35% of sub-Saharan Africa's total. (**Fact**, secondary-sourced, per [Nairametrics](https://nairametrics.com/2025/07/26/diaspora-remittances-to-nigeria-hit-20-93b-in-2024/). Canonical figure for this project: $20.93bn for 2024.)
- **Registry:** Corporate Affairs Commission, Incorporated Trustees under Part F of the Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020, sections 825 to 829. A public name search exists. Whether full organisational records are publicly queryable was not established. (**Fact** on the legal framework, **open question** on registry depth, per [Council on Foundations](https://cof.org/country-notes/nonprofit-law-nigeria).)
- **Foreign donations:** No formal restrictions on CSOs receiving foreign funding. Repeated legislative proposals over nearly two decades to increase oversight of foreign-funded NGOs have not been enacted. (**Fact**, verified, per [ICNL Nigeria](https://www.icnl.org/resources/civic-freedom-monitor/nigeria).)
- **FATF:** Clean since October 2025.
- **Payout rail (criterion 6): strong, and better suited to the target user than it first appears.** dLocal documents bank transfers into 100+ Nigerian institutions in NGN and USD, and the list extends well past traditional banks to microfinance banks and fintech providers including Moniepoint, PalmPay, and OPay. Accounts validate against the 10-digit NUBAN format with a verification algorithm. (**Fact**, primary source, [dLocal Nigeria payouts documentation](https://docs.dlocal.com/docs/nigeria-payouts-v3).)
- **[Inference]** The fintech and microfinance coverage is the important part. A small, informally-run community group in Nigeria is considerably more likely to hold an OPay or Moniepoint account than a traditional bank account, so the rail reaches the target user rather than only the formalised end of the sector. Nigeria has no wallet payout channel in dLocal's documentation, but fintech banks fill that role in practice.
- **Climate impact:** The weakest of the three on this criterion. Nigeria carries serious exposure through flooding, Lake Chad desiccation, and desertification, but does not appear near the top of the Climate Risk Index or ND-GAIN vulnerability rankings the way the other two do. (**Inference**, from its absence from those rankings.) It is included because it wins criterion 3 by a margin no other country approaches, and because losing it would remove the largest concentration of poverty in the world from a product aimed at the poor.
- **Registered climate NGO count:** Not found. Gap.

### 3. Honduras (confirmed: the hard filter it was conditional on has been cleared)
Honduras displaces Kenya on the user's own stated criteria, which was not the expected result.

- **Climate impact:** Third most affected country in the world in the Climate Risk Index long-term ranking, and third in the previous edition as well. It is the only candidate that appears in the top three of both editions, making it the strongest long-term climate-impact signal on the entire longlist. (**Fact**, per [Germanwatch CRI 2026](https://www.germanwatch.org/en/cri) for 1995 to 2024 and [CRI 2025](https://www.germanwatch.org/sites/default/files/2025-02/Climate%20Risk%20Index%202025.pdf) for 1993 to 2022.)
- **Diaspora:** Remittances reached $12.2bn in 2025, roughly 31% of GDP, up 27% year on year, overwhelmingly from Hondurans in the United States. Among the highest remittance dependencies in the world. (**Fact**, search-snippet sourced; re-verify against central bank or World Bank data.)
- **Registry:** URSAC, the Unit for Registering and Monitoring Civil Associations, operating under the 2011 NGO-D Law and its 2013 Regulations, which established a closed list of registration requirements specifically to limit official discretion. Honduras also has a Special Law for the Promotion of Non-Governmental Development Organizations that explicitly covers organisations working on environmental protection, which is directly relevant to this product's user base. (**Fact**, per [ICNL Honduras](https://www.icnl.org/resources/civic-freedom-monitor/honduras).)
- **FATF:** Not on the June 2026 list.
- **Payout rail (criterion 6): confirmed, and the weakest of the three.** dLocal documents bank transfers into 16 Honduran banks, including BAC Honduras, Banco Atlántida, Banco Ficohsa, and Banco Lafise, in HNL and USD, supporting B2C, B2B, and P2P flows. Beneficiaries must supply either a DNI (13-digit national identity number) or an RTN (14-digit tax registration number). (**Fact**, primary source, [dLocal Honduras payouts documentation](https://docs.dlocal.com/docs/honduras-payouts-v3).) **This resolves the last unverified hard filter in the selection.**
- **[Inference]** Bank transfer is the only channel, with no wallet option and 16 receiving institutions against Nigeria's 100+. An organisation therefore needs a formal bank account to be paid at all, which is exactly the barrier the product exists to lower. Honduras is viable and it is the market where the payout requirement will exclude the most target organisations.
- **Foreign donations:** **Confirmed clear.** ICNL states there are no significant legal barriers to CSOs accessing resources, including foreign funding. The hard filter is satisfied. One serious caveat applies: the government has restricted foreign donations administratively through the tax authority (SAR) during the COVID-19 pandemic and in response to natural disasters, which is precisely when this product would see peak demand. (**Fact**, verified, per [ICNL Honduras](https://www.icnl.org/resources/civic-freedom-monitor/honduras). Full treatment in `grounding-research.md` section 3.)
- **Registered climate NGO count:** Not found, and likely small in absolute terms. Weakest of the three on criterion 2.
- **Risk to weigh:** Violent crime, and a general election in November 2025 with associated pressure on civil society transparency. (**Fact** on the election timing, per [CEPR](https://cepr.net/publications/honduras-social-economic-indicators-ahead-of-2025-general-election/).)

## Conflicting statistic: resolved

Honduras' poverty rate came back with two live values in this pass: **50.5% in 2024, projected 50.3% in 2025** (World Bank Macro Poverty Outlook reporting), and **63% of the population** (other 2024 reporting). Both were surfaced rather than resolved unilaterally, per the portfolio's standing rule.

**Decision (user, August 2026): the World Bank figure is canonical. Honduras' poverty rate is 50.3%.** Every Fund the Future document uses this figure and no other. The 63% value is not to be used anywhere, including in passing. (Source: [World Bank Macro Poverty Outlook, Honduras](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/e408a7e21ba62d843bdd90dc37e61b57-0500032021/related/mpo-hnd.pdf).)

## Why Kenya was dropped, and why that was uncomfortable

Kenya scores best of any candidate on data availability, and it lost anyway.

**What Kenya wins on.** It is the only candidate where a primary-source, government-published count of registered NGOs with a sector breakdown could be obtained. As of 30 June 2023, 12,643 NGOs had been registered, of which 10,279 were active after de-registrations. Environment was the sixth largest sector by expenditure at KES 6.18bn, 5.64% of sector spending, with Water and Sanitation at 5.07% and Agriculture at 4.39% close behind. (**Fact**, primary source, extracted directly from the [Annual NGO Sector Report 2022/2023](https://www.pbora.go.ke/sites/default/files/2025-09/Annual%20NGOS%20Sector%20Report%202022-2023.pdf).) Foreign funding is legally unrestricted, with no caps or approval requirements. (**Fact**, per [ICNL Kenya](https://www.icnl.org/resources/civic-freedom-monitor/kenya).) Its mobile-money payout infrastructure is the best documented in the world.

**What killed it.** Kenya is on the FATF grey list as of June 2026, which puts heightened scrutiny and correspondent-bank de-risking directly onto the product's core operation. Separately, in 2024 the government accused 16 civil society organisations of misusing foreign funding and referred their files to the Directorate of Criminal Investigations, so foreign-funded civil society is politically exposed even though it is legally unrestricted. (**Fact**, per ICNL Kenya.) On the user's stated criteria, Honduras beats it on climate impact, poverty rate, and diaspora strength, while Kenya beats it on NGO count and registry quality.

**Criterion 6 makes this closer, and it should be said plainly.** On payout rail alone Kenya beats Honduras, because M-Pesa is the most established mobile-money payout infrastructure in the world and reaches organisations with no bank account, against Honduras' bank-transfer-only access to 16 institutions. Adding the payout criterion strengthens Kenya's case rather than weakening it.

**The selection still holds, for two reasons.** Honduras wins criteria 1, 3 and 4 (climate impact, poverty rate, diaspora), and Kenya's FATF grey-listing sits directly on the operation the payout rail performs, so a better rail into a grey-listed jurisdiction is not straightforwardly better. **Recommendation if you disagree:** Kenya is the right swap for Honduras if you weight payout maturity and registry quality above climate impact. It is the safer, better-documented market and the weaker case study.

## The finding that matters most, and it is not about country selection

Kenya's sector report contains a number that should change how the product is framed. In FY 2022/23, Kenyan NGOs received KES 196bn. **Individual donors accounted for 2.3% of it, and that source shrank 10% year on year.** The sector runs on affiliates (27.6%), other NGOs, foundations and trusts (25.8%), and foreign government agencies (21.2%). (**Fact**, primary source, same report.)

Fund the Future is a retail-giving product aimed at a sector where retail giving is currently around 2% of income and falling. That is either the opportunity, if the channel is small because no usable tool exists, or the warning, if it is small because retail donors do not fund this work. Grounding research has to answer which, because the entire product rests on the answer.

A second figure from the same report is directly relevant to the verification pillar: only 2,829 of 10,279 active NGOs filed annual reports that year. Registration status alone therefore proves less about an organisation than it appears to, even in the best-documented market on the longlist. (**Fact**, same report.)

## Countries considered and set aside
**Mozambique** ranks third on composite climate risk at 82.2, carries a very high extreme-poverty rate, and was removed from the FATF grey list in October 2025. It was set aside for a weak diaspora in higher-income markets, which is the criterion this product depends on most given its link-only distribution, and for the Cabo Delgado insurgency. It is the right choice if you want a deliberately hard-mode third market.

**Maldives**, proposed in the previous pass, does not survive a criteria-based ranking. Its foreign-funding regime is permissive and its climate exposure is existential, but a population of roughly half a million cannot compete on criterion 2 or criterion 3 against any other candidate. It was selected last time on the strength of two qualitative arguments rather than on the criteria, which is exactly the problem this document was written to fix.

**Guatemala** is the closest unranked alternative to Honduras: no restrictions on domestic CSOs receiving foreign funding (**Fact**, per [ICNL Guatemala](https://www.icnl.org/resources/civic-freedom-monitor/guatemala)), a large US diaspora, and high poverty. Its 2020 NGO Law (Decree 04-2020) complicates registration, and a reform bill has been pending since 2024.

**Madagascar, Malawi, Tanzania, Zambia, Ghana, Senegal, Vanuatu, Solomon Islands, Fiji, Indonesia, Colombia, and Peru** were on the longlist and did not reach the top three on the combined criteria.
