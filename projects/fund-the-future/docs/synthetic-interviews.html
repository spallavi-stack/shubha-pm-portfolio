Fund the Future: Synthetic Interviews

These interviews did not happen. Every person, quote and anecdote below is invented, written from the personas in personas.md and constrained by the researched facts in grounding-research.md. They are a thinking tool for pressure-testing the design and a script for the concept test that would replace them. Nothing here is evidence, and no finding below should be cited as though a real person said it. Drafted August 2026.

How they were constructed. Each interview is built by taking a persona, giving it a specific recent situation, and then working the three parts of the design that could plausibly fail for that person: the verification display, the theme's impact module, and the donation decision. Where a persona's grounding fact cuts against the product, the interview is written so the person says so rather than being led away from it. Two of the six interviews end with the person declining to use the product.

What would make these real. The product brief's success-metrics section specifies a concept test. Running it needs roughly twelve organisations drawn from the 25 live Philippine climate projects on GlobalGiving plus the 11 PCNC-accredited environment organisations, and roughly twenty donors recruited from existing environmental givers in the proposed starting markets. These transcripts are the interview guide for that work.

1. Elena Bacani, mangrove restoration, Bantayan Island

Situation given: shown the registration flow and the verification display, three weeks before her planting season.

On the missing filings. "You are going to write on the page that we have not filed for two years."

Asked how that feels. "Bad. And then I thought about it for a day. Everyone who has ever refused us knows already, because they looked it up. The only people who do not know are the ones who might have given us money. So you are telling the truth to the only group I was hiding it from." Pause. "I do not love it. I would rather you said it than made me say it."

On the cap. "So we can raise four hundred thousand, and if we file the statements we can raise more. That is the first time anyone has told me what the audit is worth in pesos. I have been treating it as a chore. It is a price."

On the theme. "Hectares and seedlings were already in the box." Asked whether that felt presumptuous. "No. It felt like someone had met a mangrove person before. The last form I filled asked me for my key performance indicator and I put 'community empowerment' because I could not think what else to write, and I knew while I was typing it that it meant nothing."

On the named person requirement. "You want my face and my name on the money." Asked if that is a problem. "It is a problem for my board, not for me. I am already the one they call. Put it up."

On what she does not believe. "You say people anywhere will find us. From where? Nobody is looking for Bantayan Island. If you are telling me I still have to bring my own people, say that on the first screen, because I have been promised an audience twice before."

What this interview is testing. Whether an organisation will accept the public display of its own non-compliance, which is untested by any source and sits directly under locked decision 6. Her last answer is the one that matters most for go-to-market, and the product does not currently have a good response to it.

2. Fr. Ruben Salazar, disaster preparedness, Guiuan

Situation given: shown theme 6 and its impact module, a year after his foundation funder had no surplus.

On the unit. "Drills run. Households with a plan. People trained." Long pause. "I have run four hundred and ten drills. I have never written that number anywhere, because nobody asked for it and I did not think it was a number. It was just Tuesdays."

On whether the number persuades. "It will not persuade anyone who is not already thinking about it. Look, when the water comes, the photograph is the roof. There is no photograph of the roof that did not come off." Asked what he would show instead. "The evacuation list. Two hundred and six names, and where each one goes, and who checks on them. That is the real thing. Can your page hold a list?"

On the costed budget. "This is the part I can do. Fuel for the boat, the radio units, the tarpaulin for the assembly point, the food for the volunteers on drill day. I know every peso of it because I have begged for every peso of it separately."

On the wait. "Five days is nothing. The last application took eleven months and then said no."

On what he does not believe. "You have not solved my problem. My problem is that people give after. You have made it easier for me to ask before, which is good, and you have not made anybody want to answer."

What this interview is testing. The hardest case in the theme library, and the product's deepest open assumption. His last answer is the honest statement of it: the product improves supply-side legibility and does nothing to the demand-side preference for visible emergencies. His request for a list, rather than a count, is a real design input for the impact module.

3. Marisol Ocampo, unregistered shoreline waste group, Navotas

Situation given: shown the eligibility check, which rejects her.

On the rejection. "One minute. That is genuinely better than the others. GlobalGiving took me through four screens before it told me." Asked whether the reason was clear. "Yes. SEC registration. It even told me it was the only thing missing, which means someone thought about what to say."

On what she would want next. "Tell me the cost. Not 'you need to register', but 'it costs this much, it takes this long, here is who does it'. You already know, because you check the register all day. That is the difference between a door closing and a door with a sign on it."

On the underlying issue. "Four years, every Saturday, forty people. And the rule is a piece of paper." Asked if the rule seems wrong. "No. If anyone can put up a page and take money, someone will do it who is not us, and then nobody trusts any of us. I understand the rule. I just want you to admit that the rule is why you cannot help me, instead of pretending you are for everyone."

What this interview is testing. Whether the rejection path is a designed experience. Her second answer is a concrete product change worth making: the rejection screen should carry the cost and duration of SEC registration and a route to it, since the platform knows the register intimately and the information is free.

4. Anders Holm, donor, Utrecht

Situation given: sent a campaign link by a colleague. Reads the page unprompted.

On the first ten seconds. "Photograph, name, amount. Fine. I scroll past all of it." Asked what he is looking for. "The number. There is always a registration number somewhere or there is not, and if there is not I am finished."

On the verification section. "So this is registered, active, the officer sheet was filed in March, and the audited accounts were not filed for two years." Asked what he concludes. "That they are small and disorganised, which I assumed. What I now also know is that you know, and you published it anyway. That is a much stronger signal than a green tick would have been. A tick tells me you have a standard. This tells me what your standard is."

On checking it himself. He opens the SEC search in a second tab and types the number. "Right, it matches." Asked whether he would have given without checking. "Probably. But I would not have given to the next one."

On the budget. "Seedlings, nursery labour, boat fuel, six months of a coordinator at a stated rate. I can add this up and it adds up." Asked about the coordinator line. "Good. If you had hidden the salary I would have assumed you were hiding more."

On what stops him. "No deduction. In the Netherlands I get relief on periodic gifts to recognised bodies and this is not one." Asked how much that matters. "It costs me about a third. It does not stop me at fifty euros. It would stop me at a thousand, and a thousand is the size I actually give at."

What this interview is testing. Whether structured disclosure substitutes for audited financials, which is untested by any source and belongs in the concept test. His last answer is the sharpest statement of the deductibility trade in the project: the product may be viable at small gift sizes and structurally excluded at the sizes this donor segment actually gives.

5. Priya Nair-Whitcombe, donor, Bristol

Situation given: arrives on a campaign page from a friend's message, on a phone, in the evening.

On the two buttons. "Once or monthly, both there. Good." Asked why. "Because if you show me monthly afterwards I will have already finished and I will say no. You get one moment."

On the amount. "Twenty a month. It is what I give to everything." Asked whether the suggested amounts influenced her. "They were in pesos and pounds. I looked at the pounds."

On the reporting commitment. "'We will send photographs of the planted area and a spend report in April.' " Asked whether she believes it. "No, not really. Everyone says that. But it is written down before I paid, which is different from an email afterwards saying thank you. If April comes and nothing arrives, I will cancel, and I will be right to."

On the tip. "You are asking me for money for you, on top." Asked whether that is objectionable. "It is honest. I would rather that than you taking it out of theirs without telling me. Leave it at whatever it defaults to, I am not going to think about it."

On what worries her. "If they stop existing, what happens to my standing order? Who tells me? On my UK ones the bank tells me, and there is a charity commission to complain to. Here there is you, and you have just told me you do not hold the money."

What this interview is testing. Whether recurring giving is accepted at the same decision point, and whether donor tips survive being seen. Her last answer names a real gap: the product has no story for what happens to a recurring donor when an organisation's green light lapses at twelve months or when its SEC status changes, and that is a roadmap item rather than a caveat.

6. Tomas Reyes-Mbeki, donor, Toronto

Situation given: arrives at the platform directly, with no link, after reading a news story. Uses the Matchmaker.

On arriving. "I did not come from anywhere. I typed something and this appeared. So the first thing I need is for you to not make me choose between forty things."

On the filter. "Kind of work, and how much is still needed. That is two questions I can answer." Asked whether he wanted a recommendation. "I wanted one and I am glad you did not give me one, because I would not have trusted it. If you had put one at the top I would have assumed you were paid to."

On what he picked. "The rainwater one, because two hundred and forty households is a number I can hold. The mangrove one was hectares and I do not know what a hectare is."

On the amount. "Forty dollars. It is not much." Asked whether he expected to give more. "No. I expected to give nothing, which is what I have done every other time. The difference is that this took four minutes and I know what it bought."

On what he does not want. "Do not email me about the crisis. If you send me a thing every week about how bad it is I will unsubscribe, because that is exactly the feeling I came here to get rid of. Send me the photograph of the tank."

What this interview is testing. Whether the Matchmaker converts a donor who arrives without a link, and whether ordering on donor-chosen facts holds up against the expectation of a recommendation. Two design inputs come out of it: units the donor already understands beat units native to the work, which cuts against the theme library's premise for at least one theme, and the retention message has to be evidence of completion rather than restatement of the crisis.

What the six interviews would change if they were real

Five things, ordered by how much they would cost to act on.

  1. The rejection screen carries the cost, the duration and a route to SEC registration. Cheap, and the platform already holds the knowledge. From interview 3.
  2. The retention message is a photograph of the finished thing, not a restatement of the problem. Free, and it contradicts the default instinct of the category. From interviews 5 and 6.
  3. The impact module supports a list as well as a count. Ruben's two hundred and six names are more persuasive than his four hundred and ten drills, and the design brief left the module's form deliberately open. From interview 2.
  4. The theme's native unit is not always the donor's unit. Hectares are correct and illegible. A theme may need to carry a donor-facing translation alongside its own unit, which is a real addition to the theme model rather than a copy change. From interview 6.
  5. The product has no answer for a lapsed green light against a live recurring donation. Twelve-month expiry and SEC status change are both recorded as decisions, and neither has a donor-side consequence designed. From interview 5. This is the largest gap the six interviews surface.

What the six interviews cannot do

They are written by the same process that designed the product, so they cannot falsify it. Every objection above is one that was already reachable by thinking, which is exactly why they are a design tool rather than evidence. The three questions that decide the product are untestable this way and are recorded as such in the handover's worklist: whether donors accept structured disclosure in place of audited financials, whether the identifiable-victim page structure conflicts with the effectiveness finding, and whether individual donors will fund adaptation specifically rather than disaster relief. Interviews 2 and 4 are written so their central character voices the doubt rather than resolving it, which is the most these documents can honestly do.
