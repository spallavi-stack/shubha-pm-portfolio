Where a Fact was obtained from a search snippet rather than the primary document, it says so and carries a verification task.

## 1. The adaptation funding gap

**[Fact]** Adaptation finance needs in developing countries are projected at US$310bn to US$365bn per year by 2035. International public adaptation finance flows stood at US$26bn in 2023. Needs are therefore 12 to 14 times current flows. (Source: primary, [UNEP Adaptation Gap Report 2025, "Running on Empty"](https://www.unep.org/resources/adaptation-gap-report-2025), UNEP's own publication page read 21 August 2026. An earlier pass recorded unep.org and four secondary sites as returning HTTP 403 to automated fetching and flagged these figures as needing a human; the page was reachable in this pass and every figure matched what the snippets had reported. The $310bn is the modelled cost and the $365bn the extrapolation from Nationally Determined Contributions and National Adaptation Plans.)

**[Fact]** UNEP states the adaptation finance gap itself as **US$284bn to US$339bn per year until 2035**. (Same source.)

**[Inference]** That single annualised figure is the one to use in public-facing writing, and the 12-to-14-times ratio is the one to avoid. The ratio is UNEP's own, but it is built by comparing a 2035 need against a 2023 flow, and a reader who is not told so will read it as a same-year comparison. The gap figure carries the same force with no year mismatch inside it. (Framing decided by the user, 22 August 2026.)

**[Fact]** UNEP puts the private sector's realistic contribution at around US$50bn per year, conditional on targeted policy action and blended finance. (Same source.)

**[Fact]** The Glasgow Climate Pact goal of doubling international public adaptation finance from 2019 levels by 2025 will not be met on current trends, and the New Collective Quantified Goal is not sized to close the gap. (Same source.)

### How little of it reaches local actors

**[Fact]** Across climate finance reported for 2016 to 2023, **0.17% mentioned "locally led" or "community led"**, rising to 0.39% in 2023. Over the same period 29% of total funding mentioned local impact or involvement, rising from 26.1% in 2016 to 33.7% in 2023. For adaptation funding specifically, mentions of local people rose from 25.5% in 2016 to 39.2% in 2023. Only 0.7% of all reported climate finance mentioned Indigenous peoples. (Source: [IIED](https://www.iied.org/climate-finance-not-reaching-local-level), **verified directly against the primary source**.)

**[Fact]** IIED's earlier 2017 analysis found less than 10% of international climate fund commitments were directed at the local level, and that analysis covered only the 7% of climate finance transparent enough to track at all. (Same source, verified directly.)

**[Inference]** These figures measure how climate finance *describes* itself, because the method counts keyword mentions in finance descriptions. They are therefore an upper bound on local targeting rather than a measurement of where money lands: funding that mentions local involvement may still terminate above the community level. The direction is unambiguous and the precision is not. Any public-facing document should use the 0.17% figure with its method stated, since "0.17% of climate finance reaches local organisations" would be a stronger claim than the source supports.

**[Inference]** Adaptation is underfunded in aggregate and badly distributed, and those are two separate failures. US$26bn per year does flow, and almost none of it is described in terms that suggest it reaches community-level actors. Fund the Future addresses the distribution failure only. This matters for positioning, because the product is a distribution mechanism and claiming it addresses the adaptation finance gap as a whole would overstate it.

**[Inference]** The corollary is that the product's real competitor is the institutional grant channel these organisations cannot access, rather than other crowdfunding platforms. The question that decides the opportunity is whether retail giving can be made to work for this sector at all, which is a different question from whether this platform beats GoFundMe.

## 2. Market sizing beyond historical adoption

Per the standing rule that market sizing does not stop at historical volumes, three angles were checked.

**Addressable population, by pilot market.**

**[Fact]** Philippines: 64,087 NPOs registered across classifications as of 31 December 2020. (Source: Philippine SEC data, search-snippet sourced.)

**[Fact] No current count is published, and the SEC does not produce one on request.** Answering a March 2025 Freedom of Information request for the total number of registered non-stock/non-profit organisations from 2016 to 2023, the SEC did not supply a count. It directed the requester to its Information Systems Development and Management Division and quoted a special programme fee of PHP 1,000 plus a computer processing charge of PHP 4.00 per minute for a data extract containing corporation name, SEC registration number, date registered and company address. (Source: primary, [eFOI request to the SEC](https://www.foi.gov.ph/agencies/sec/number-of-non-stocknon-profit-orgs-in-the-philippines/), verified 21 August 2026. The linked AMLC NPO Risk Assessment PDF now returns HTTP 404.)

**[Inference]** The register holds the data and will sell an extract of it, and publishes no aggregate. A current sector count is therefore obtainable but not free, and what it would return is a raw list to be counted rather than a figure to be quoted. For an MVP case study the 2020 figure with its date stated is adequate, and the paid extract is the route if a current number is ever needed.

**[Fact]** Kenya, which is not a pilot market but is the only country in this research where a government-published sector breakdown could be obtained: 12,643 NGOs registered by 30 June 2023, of which 10,279 were active. Environment was the sixth largest sector by expenditure at KES 6.18bn, 5.64% of sector spending, with Water and Sanitation at 5.07% and Agriculture at 4.39%. (Source: primary, [Annual NGO Sector Report 2022/2023](https://www.pbora.go.ke/sites/default/files/2025-09/Annual%20NGOS%20Sector%20Report%202022-2023.pdf).)

**[Inference]** Applying Kenya's environment share as a rough proxy, climate and environment organisations are on the order of 5% of a registered NGO sector, with adjacent water and agriculture work adding perhaps another 9%. Against the Philippines' 64,087 registered NPOs that would imply roughly 3,000 environment-focused organisations, or closer to 9,000 counting water and agriculture. This is a cross-country proxy applied to a different regulatory regime and should be treated as an order-of-magnitude estimate only.

**A Philippine sector breakdown, at the certified tier.** **[Fact]** The Philippine Council for NGO Certification accredits 604 organisations and publishes them with a sector category. Of those, **11 are categorised Environment/Biodiversity**, against 306 Social welfare and development, 117 in the education categories and 36 Health. Searching the register for "disaster" returns 2 organisations across all 604. (Source: primary, the [PCNC register of accredited NGOs](https://pcnc.com.ph/accredited-ngos/), read 21 August 2026 by filtering the register's own search.)

**[Inference]** Environment is 1.8% of accredited organisations, against the 5.64% of sector expenditure that environment takes in Kenya. The two numbers measure different things, one a count of certified organisations and the other a share of money spent, so the gap is not a contradiction. What the PCNC figure adds is a floor rather than an estimate: it counts organisations that cleared an accreditation bar, and the named entries skew heavily to large, corporate-backed or long-established foundations. It says the professionalised end of Philippine environmental civil society is small in absolute terms, roughly a dozen organisations, and says nothing about the population below it.

**[Inference]** That reads directly onto the product. PCNC accreditation is close to the documentation floor Fund the Future exists to reach below, so 11 is approximately the number of Philippine environmental organisations that do **not** need this product. It also means the two organisations on the whole register doing disaster or resilience work by name are the certified tier of exactly the category the product serves, which is thin enough to reinforce the fundable-set question in open question 1.

**[Assumption]** No count of registered climate or environment NGOs, as distinct from accredited ones, was found for the Philippines. Criterion 2 of the market selection is now partly satisfied at the accredited tier and remains unsatisfied at the registered tier.

**Informal-sector flag.** **[Inference]** Registered counts systematically undercount this product's target user, because the product exists for organisations too small or too informal to compete for institutional finance, which are the organisations least likely to appear on a register. In Kenya, only 2,829 of 10,279 active NGOs filed annual reports in FY 2022/23 (**[Fact]**, same primary source), so even registration itself is a weak signal of an organisation being active and documented. The true addressable population is larger than any registry count and is not directly measurable.

**Comparable market with a head start.** **[Fact]** Foundation funding for climate adaptation and resilience reached an estimated $870m in 2024, more than double the $404m tracked in 2021, and the number of foundations making adaptation-related grants grew 55%. (Source: [ClimateWorks](https://www.climateworks.org/press-release/funding-for-climate-adaptation-and-resilience-hits-record-of-870-million/). Full treatment in section 13.)

**[Inference]** This is the closest thing to a proven market for what the product sells. It is institutional rather than retail money, so it does not demonstrate individual willingness to give, but it establishes that adaptation work is fundable, that funders are entering the category rather than leaving it, and that the constraint on the category is supply of money rather than absence of fundable organisations.

**Forward-looking target.** **[Fact]** Total climate mitigation funding from foundations and individuals reached $11.7bn to $18.4bn in 2024, about 2.1% of total global philanthropic giving, crossing the 2% threshold for the first time. (Source: [ClimateWorks Funding Trends](https://www.climateworks.org/report/funding-trends-2026/).)

**[Inference]** Climate philanthropy as a whole is growing as a share of all giving, and adaptation is the fastest-growing slice of it from the smallest base. A product entering now is entering a category in expansion. The size of that category, at under $1bn for adaptation, also caps how large this product can plausibly become on its current framing.

## 3. Pilot market profiles

### Philippines
- **[Fact]** Poverty incidence among population was 15.5% in 2023, equal to 17.54 million Filipinos, down from 19.99 million in 2021. Poverty incidence among families was 10.9%, or 3.0 million families. (Source: [Philippine Statistics Authority, 2023 Full Year Official Poverty Statistics](https://psa.gov.ph/sites/default/files/phdsd/2023%20FY%20Official%20Poverty%20Statistics%20Publication_15August2024.pdf).) **Canonical figure for this project: 15.5% and 17.54 million.** PSA also publishes a first-semester 2023 figure of 22.4% and 25.24 million, which is a different measure covering a different period. To prevent the same fact carrying two numbers, the full-year figure is the only one used in Fund the Future documents.
- **[Fact]** Among the ten most affected countries in the Climate Risk Index long-term ranking, and one of five lower-middle-income countries in that top ten. (Source: [Germanwatch CRI 2025](https://www.germanwatch.org/sites/default/files/2025-02/Climate%20Risk%20Index%202025.pdf).)
- **[Fact]** Foreign donations are permitted. Receiving foreign grants triggers additional SEC, BIR, and AMLA reporting obligations rather than an approval gate, and does not change the entity's Philippine character. Foreign NPOs may not fund political parties, candidates, or partisan political activity. (Source: [Council on Foundations, Nonprofit Law in the Philippines, June 2025](https://cof.org/sites/default/files/2025-08/Nonprofit-Law-in-Philippines.pdf).)
- **[Fact]** NPOs are defined and regulated under SEC Memorandum Circular 25 series of 2019, and must file annual General Information Sheets and audited financial statements. (Same source.)
- **[Fact]** Removed from the FATF grey list in February 2025 after nearly four years. (Source: [The Diplomat](https://thediplomat.com/2025/02/philippines-removed-from-watchdogs-money-laundering-grey-list/).)

### Nigeria
- **[Fact]** Holds the largest absolute number of people in extreme poverty of any country, roughly 12% of the global total, and is projected together with DR Congo to account for a quarter of global extreme poverty by 2030. (Source: World Bank reporting, search-snippet sourced; verify against the [Nigeria Poverty and Equity Brief, October 2025](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099253204222517873/txt/IDU-3e010784-3371-4dcb-b923-112e98052ed9.txt).)
- **[Fact]** No formal restrictions on CSOs receiving foreign funding. Repeated legislative proposals over nearly two decades to increase government oversight of foreign-funded NGOs have not been enacted. Officials have historically labelled critical CSOs foreign-sponsored, though ICNL reports this has diminished. (Source: [ICNL Nigeria Civic Freedom Monitor](https://www.icnl.org/resources/civic-freedom-monitor/nigeria).)
- **[Fact]** NGOs register as Incorporated Trustees under Part F of the Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020, sections 825 to 829, administered by the Corporate Affairs Commission. (Source: [Council on Foundations, Nigeria](https://cof.org/country-notes/nonprofit-law-nigeria).)
- **[Fact]** Removed from the FATF grey list in October 2025, alongside South Africa, Mozambique, and Burkina Faso. (Source: [Aluko & Oyebode](https://www.aluko-oyebode.com/insights/nigeria-fatf-grey-list-removal-2025/).)

### Honduras
- **[Fact]** Poverty is projected at 50.5% in 2024 and 50.3% in 2025. (Source: [World Bank Macro Poverty Outlook, Honduras](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/e408a7e21ba62d843bdd90dc37e61b57-0500032021/related/mpo-hnd.pdf).) **Canonical figure for this project: 50.3%**, per user decision, August 2026. A 63% figure circulates in other 2024 reporting and is not used anywhere in this project.
- **[Fact]** Third most affected country in the world in the Climate Risk Index long-term ranking for 1995 to 2024, and third in the previous edition covering 1993 to 2022. It is the only country on this project's longlist appearing in the top three of both editions. (Sources: [Germanwatch CRI 2026](https://www.germanwatch.org/en/cri), [CRI 2025](https://www.germanwatch.org/sites/default/files/2025-02/Climate%20Risk%20Index%202025.pdf).)
- **[Fact]** There are no formal legal restrictions on foreign funding for civil society organisations. ICNL states: "While there are no significant legal barriers to accessing resources, in practice CSOs, particularly those providing humanitarian assistance, have faced serious obstacles." (Source: [ICNL Honduras Civic Freedom Monitor](https://www.icnl.org/resources/civic-freedom-monitor/honduras).) **This clears the hard filter that Honduras' inclusion was conditional on.**
- **[Fact]** The obstacles are administrative and discretionary rather than codified. ICNL: "During the COVID-19 pandemic and in response to natural disasters, organizations have reported difficulties providing aid to affected communities. Instead of facilitating their efforts, the government has imposed taxes and restricted foreign donations through the Ministry of Finance and Administration and Income Services (SAR)." (Same source.)
- **[Inference]** This is the most product-relevant finding in the Honduras profile and it cuts against the product directly. Honduras restricts foreign donations administratively **during natural disasters**, which is precisely when a climate-adaptation and disaster-response fundraising platform would see peak demand. A campaign platform whose traffic spikes after a hurricane would meet its worst payout friction at exactly that moment. This belongs in the product brief as a named risk, and any roadmap should assume payouts can stall during the events that drive the fundraising.
- **[Fact]** Organisations register with URSAC, the Unit for Registering and Monitoring Civil Associations, under the 2011 NGO-D Law and its 2013 Regulations, which created a closed list of registration requirements specifically to limit official discretion in granting legal personality. A Special Law for the Promotion of Non-Governmental Development Organizations covers organisations working on environmental protection and human rights. (Same source.)
- **[Fact]** Not on the FATF list of jurisdictions under increased monitoring as of June 2026.

## 4. Platform-side regulation

**[Fact]** Donation-based and rewards-based crowdfunding are generally outside securities regulation, unlike debt and equity crowdfunding. (Source: [Library of Congress fintech research guide](https://guides.loc.gov/fintech/21st-century/crowdfunding).)

**[Fact]** A platform that collects funds from donors and distributes them to recipients may be classified as a money transmitter. In the United States, money transmitters are a category of Money Services Business under the Bank Secrecy Act, must register with FinCEN, and require state-level licensing in 47 states plus the District of Columbia. (Sources: [Library of Congress](https://guides.loc.gov/fintech/21st-century/crowdfunding), [Revision Legal on payment facilitator regulation](https://revisionlegal.com/saas/payment-facilitator-regulations/).)

**[Inference]** This is the single largest structural decision in the product and it is a legal one rather than a technical one. If Fund the Future holds donor funds and then disburses them, it is likely a money transmitter in its donor markets, which means US state-by-state licensing before it can operate at all. If instead each campaign's payments are processed directly into an account belonging to the organisation, through a licensed payment provider whose regulated entity does the transmitting, the platform is a software layer and avoids that classification. The second architecture is almost certainly the viable one for a 0-to-1 product, and it constrains the whole design: the platform never touches the money, which also means it cannot hold funds in escrow pending verification, cannot easily offer its own refund guarantee, and cannot pool small donations.

**[Assumption]** The above reasoning is drawn from general US money transmission principles and has not been verified against a specific legal analysis of donation platforms. It is load-bearing enough that it should be flagged as an open question in the product brief rather than stated as settled.

**[Fact]** FATF Recommendation 8 covers the nonprofit sector's exposure to terrorist financing abuse. Overzealous implementation has led banks to de-risk NPOs en masse, and states to impose blanket measures including onerous licensing and registration requirements. FATF assessments have found that terrorist abuse of NPOs is infrequent relative to the size of the sector, and FATF revised Recommendation 8 in 2023 and launched a project in February 2021 specifically to address unintended consequences including de-risking and financial exclusion. (Sources: [RUSI](https://www.rusi.org/explore-our-research/publications/commentary/fatfs-recommendation-8-cure-worse-disease), [Global NPO Coalition on FATF](https://fatfplatform.org/context/fatf-and-npos/), [FATF](https://www.fatf-gafi.org/en/topics/non-profit-organisations.html).)

**[Inference]** Nonprofit financial exclusion is a documented, acknowledged, regulator-recognised problem rather than an edge case. That is good news for the product's premise, because it means the barrier Fund the Future targets is real and named by the standard-setter itself. It is bad news for execution, because a platform whose entire business is routing money to small nonprofits in developing countries sits precisely where banks have chosen to reduce exposure. The platform will inherit its payment provider's risk appetite, and that appetite is the binding constraint rather than the platform's own policy.

## 5. Payments and payout rails

This was scoped as the hard problem and the research confirms it is.

**[Fact]** Stripe supports merchants in roughly 46 countries, concentrated in Europe and North America, with selective coverage elsewhere. Nigeria appears as an extended-network country via Paystack, which Stripe owns. (Source: [Rapyd's Stripe country analysis](https://www.rapyd.net/blog/are-you-located-in-a-stripe-supported-country/), [Stripe global availability](https://stripe.com/global).)

**[Fact] Upgraded to primary, and it is worse than recorded.** Stripe's own Connect documentation limits cross-border payout recipients to a set of high-income jurisdictions covering the United States, the United Kingdom, the EEA, Canada and Switzerland, with anything outside that requiring a sales conversation. **None of the Philippines, Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana or Honduras is a supported recipient country.** (Source: primary, [Stripe Connect cross-border payouts](https://docs.stripe.com/connect/cross-border-payouts), read 21 August 2026.)

**[Inference]** The distinction that matters, and that the scope named in advance, is confirmed: accepting a donation and paying out to the organisation are different capabilities with different country coverage. Stripe Connect is the standard architecture for a marketplace or platform paying out to third parties, and the primary documentation rules out every market this project has considered, not just Nigeria. The default architecture a developer would reach for cannot pay this product's users at all, which is why dLocal and Xendit are load-bearing rather than optional, and why the platform integrates a different rail per market rather than one global one.

**[Fact]** Philippine domestic gateways include PayMongo and Dragonpay, supporting cards, e-wallets, online banking, and over-the-counter cash payment at convenience stores, supermarkets, and rural pawnshops. GCash and Maya are the dominant e-wallets, with Maya reporting 50 million registered users as of June 2022. (Sources: [HitPay gateway comparison](https://hitpayapp.com/blog/philippines-payment-gateway-comparison), [Wise on Dragonpay](https://wise.com/ph/blog/what-is-dragonpay), [Maya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_(mobile_payments)).)

**[Inference]** Domestic gateways such as PayMongo and Dragonpay solve a different problem from the one this product has. They let a Philippine merchant accept payments, mostly from Philippine payers. Fund the Future needs to accept payment from a donor abroad and settle to a Philippine organisation, which is a cross-border flow. Domestic acquiring does not provide that on its own, and the over-the-counter cash channel, while impressive for domestic financial inclusion, is irrelevant to a Global North donor.

### Verified payout coverage, all three markets

Payout rail was upgraded from a hard filter to a scored selection criterion, and each pilot market was then verified against primary provider documentation rather than assumed.

**[Fact]** **Philippines.** dLocal documents both bank transfers and real-time wallet transfers, in PHP and USD. Supported wallets include GCash, PayMaya, Coins, GrabPay, Starpay, TayoCash, and PalawanPay. Wallet transfers are capped at PHP 50,000 per transaction. B2C, B2B, and P2P flows are supported. Bank transfers require a PSN document number in 12-digit format. (Source: primary, [dLocal Philippines payouts documentation](https://docs.dlocal.com/docs/philippines-payouts-v3).)

**[Fact]** **Nigeria.** Bank transfers only, in NGN and USD, reaching 100+ institutions. The list includes traditional banks (First Bank, UBA, GTBank, Access, Zenith, Fidelity) alongside microfinance banks and fintech providers including Moniepoint, PalmPay, and OPay. Accounts validate against the 10-digit NUBAN format with a verification algorithm. (Source: primary, [dLocal Nigeria payouts documentation](https://docs.dlocal.com/docs/nigeria-payouts-v3).)

**[Fact]** **Honduras.** Bank transfers only, in HNL and USD, reaching 16 banks including BAC Honduras, Banco Atlántida, Banco Ficohsa, Banco Azteca, and Banco Lafise. B2C, B2B, and P2P flows are supported. Beneficiaries must supply either a DNI, the 13-digit national identity number, or an RTN, the 14-digit tax registration number. (Source: primary, [dLocal Honduras payouts documentation](https://docs.dlocal.com/docs/honduras-payouts-v3).) **This resolves the last unverified hard filter in the market selection.**

**[Inference]** Ranked on how established the rail is, the order is Philippines, then Nigeria, then Honduras. The Philippines has two channel types and reaches organisations with no bank account through wallets. Nigeria has one channel type but its 100+ institutions extend to fintech and microfinance providers, which a small informally-run group is more likely to hold than a traditional bank account, so the rail reaches the target user despite having no wallet option. Honduras has one channel type and 16 institutions, all conventional banks, so an organisation there needs a formal bank account to receive anything. Honduras is viable and it is the market where the payout requirement itself will exclude the most target organisations.

**[Inference]** A finding that cuts across the verification pillar: every market's payout documentation requires a beneficiary document number tied to a person or a legal entity, whether that is a Philippine PSN, a Nigerian NUBAN-validated account, or a Honduran DNI or RTN. Verification is therefore not a feature the platform chooses to add. The rail imposes an identity floor before it will move money, and the platform's verification ladder starts from that floor rather than from zero. Nigeria's NUBAN algorithm and account-name resolution are a free verification signal the product gets from the rail itself.

### Second provider per market, verified

The first pass established rail existence through a single vendor, dLocal, which proves a rail exists and not that the product is independent of one company. A second provider was verified per market against its own documentation.

**[Fact]** **Philippines: Xendit.** Disburses to 94+ banks and 13 e-wallets in the Philippines, with channel codes including PH_GCASH, PH_PAYMAYA, PH_COINS, and PH_GRABPAY, plus 450+ banks across South East Asia. Transfers run daily including holidays via direct bank integrations. Xendit also offers Payout Links, which let a sender disburse without collecting the recipient's bank details up front by having the recipient choose their own receiving method through a secure link. (Source: [Xendit xenPayout](https://www.xendit.co/en-ph/products/xenpayout/), [channel codes documentation](https://docs.xendit.co/id/xendisburse/channel-codes).)

**[Fact]** **Nigeria: Flutterwave.** Transfers from a Flutterwave balance to Nigerian bank accounts in NGN, addressed by bank code and account number, with instant, deferred, and scheduled processing modes. USD transfers to Nigerian domiciliary accounts are supported. Merchant KYC approval and server IP whitelisting are required before any transfer can run. Flutterwave additionally covers mobile money across Ghana, Uganda, Rwanda, Zambia, and M-Pesa. (Source: [Flutterwave bank transfer documentation](https://developer.flutterwave.com/docs/bank-transfer), [Nigerian bank account transfer](https://developer.flutterwave.com/v3.0/docs/nigerian-bank-account-transfer).)

**[Fact]** **Honduras: Nium.** Two channels. International wire transfer over SWIFT, in USD, EUR, GBP, SGD, AUD, CAD, HKD, JPY, CHF, and NZD, with no maximum limit and same-day processing subject to a 07:30 UTC cutoff. Visa card payout in HNL, real-time for fast-funds-enabled cards and T+2 otherwise, capped at USD 2,500 per transaction for P2P and USD 50,000 for B2B, B2P, and P2B. Wire transfers require beneficiary name, account type and number, country, address, city, and postcode. Card payouts require only card number, name, and country. (Source: [Nium Honduras playbook](https://playbook.nium.com/country/honduras).)

**[Inference] Two markets have genuine redundancy and one does not.** Xendit against dLocal in the Philippines, and Flutterwave against dLocal in Nigeria, are like-for-like substitutions: same destination type, same local currency, same domestic rails. Either provider could be swapped for the other without changing what the recipient does to get paid.

**[Inference] Honduras is redundant in name only, and the fallback is materially worse.** dLocal pays HNL into a local bank account. Nium's wire channel settles in USD and other major currencies over SWIFT rather than into a local HNL account, and SWIFT wire economics are poor at donation scale, where a fixed correspondent-bank fee can consume a large share of a small payout. Nium's card channel does pay in HNL, and it pays a card rather than a bank account, so the recipient needs a different instrument. Honduras therefore has a second provider without having a comparable second rail, and a dLocal outage or an onboarding rejection there would degrade the service rather than reroute it cleanly.

**[Inference] One upside hidden in that finding.** Nium's HNL card payout may suit the target user better than either bank rail. A Visa debit card is easier for a small informal organisation to obtain than a business bank account, and the beneficiary requirements are lighter, needing only card number, name, and country against the wire channel's full address details. The P2P cap of USD 2,500 per transaction is well above typical campaign payout sizes. This is worth testing rather than assuming, since card-based payout could lower the documentation floor in the market where that floor currently excludes the most organisations.

**[Assumption]** Rapyd and Thunes were checked as further alternatives and neither publishes country-level payout coverage in a form that could be verified without a sales conversation. Rapyd's public documentation describes payout method categories without a country list, and Thunes states 140+ country coverage without published per-country detail. Both may well cover these markets. Neither can be cited as evidence.

### The stacked cost

**[Fact]** In Q3 2025 the global average cost of sending a US$200 remittance was **6.36%**, down from 6.49% the previous quarter, against a UN Sustainable Development Goal target of 3% by 2030. Sub-Saharan Africa is the most expensive receiving region at **8.46%**. Digital remittances averaged **4.59%** and non-digital 7.30%. Sending to G20 receiving markets averaged 5.68%. (Source: primary, quoted directly from [World Bank Remittance Prices Worldwide, Issue 54](https://remittanceprices.worldbank.org/sites/default/files/2026-04/RPW_main_report_and_annex_Q325.pdf), report PDF read 21 August 2026.)

**Numeric conflict: resolved.** An earlier pass recorded 6.49% global, 8.78% for Sub-Saharan Africa and 3.65% for digital, all search-snippet sourced. The report itself gives 6.36%, 8.46% and 4.59% for Q3 2025. **Decision (user, August 2026): the Q3 2025 primary set is canonical.** The superseded figures are not to be used anywhere in this project's material.

**[Fact]** GoFundMe charges 2.9% plus US$0.30 per donation, or 2.2% plus US$0.30 for verified charities, with no platform fee, relying instead on optional donor contributions. (Source: primary, [GoFundMe pricing](https://www.gofundme.com/c/pricing), verified 21 August 2026.)

**[Inference]** Stacking the components for a US$50 donation from a donor in a high-income country to an organisation in a pilot market: card processing at roughly 2.9% plus US$0.30 takes about US$1.75, or 3.5%. Cross-border settlement and FX, using remittance pricing as the closest available proxy, takes between 4.59% at digital-provider rates and 8.46% at sub-Saharan African averages. Before Fund the Future charges anything at all, the donation has lost somewhere between **8% and 12%**. A "small platform fee" of 3% to 5% on top would put total leakage between 11% and 17%, which is part of why the fee model was reversed to donor tips.

**[Inference]** Remittance pricing is a proxy and not a like-for-like measure, because a platform moving bulk volume through a single payment provider should achieve better rates than a retail sender at a money transfer operator. The direction of the estimate is more reliable than its precision. What the estimate establishes is that cross-border cost, not the platform fee, is the dominant term, and that fee transparency to donors will be uncomfortable regardless of how small the platform's own cut is.

**[Inference]** Fixed per-transaction fees punish small donations disproportionately. At US$0.30 per transaction, a US$5 donation loses 6% to the fixed component alone before any percentage applies. A product designed around many small retail gifts is structurally worse off than one designed around fewer larger ones, which argues for donation-size nudges or batching in the design.

## 6. Competitive landscape

### The closest analog: GlobalGiving

**[Fact]** GlobalGiving connects nonprofits to donors across more than 175 countries. (Source: [GlobalGiving](https://www.globalgiving.org/nonprofits/).)

**[Fact]** Its fee structure is a nonprofit support fee varying from 12% down to 5% by amount raised and country of registration, with US- and UK-registered organisations at 5% and organisations registered elsewhere at 7%, plus 3% third-party processing on all donations. GlobalGiving guarantees a minimum of 85 cents of every dollar donated reaches the nonprofit. Disbursement amounts exclude currency conversion and wire transfer fees. (Sources: [GlobalGiving fee page](https://www.globalgiving.org/aboutus/fee/), [reduced fee details](https://www.globalgiving.org/reduced-fee-details/), [support article](https://support.globalgiving.org/hc/en-us/articles/360033731831-What-is-GlobalGiving-s-fee).)

**[Fact]** Every organisation is vetted through an online due diligence process. GlobalGiving collects and reviews legal documents, financial records, programme materials, and lists of senior staff and board members. Financial requirements include a recent tax filing and/or two years of financial statements, plus a current-year budget showing planned income and expenses. It verifies compliance with local government registration requirements, screens against anti-terror guidelines and international philanthropy guidelines, evaluates the organisation's capacity to implement activities and communicate about its work, and researches its relationship with previous funders. Applications are submitted by invitation, within 21 days of receiving one. (Sources: [GlobalGiving nonprofit application documents](https://www.globalgiving.org/nonprofit-application-documents/), [how a nonprofit can join](https://support.globalgiving.org/hc/en-us/articles/39772852954132-How-can-a-nonprofit-join-GlobalGiving), [what the application needs](https://support.globalgiving.org/hc/en-us/articles/360026454991-What-do-I-need-for-the-application)).

**[Inference] What GlobalGiving does not do, which is the actual gap.** GlobalGiving is not failing to serve the Global South. It operates in 175 countries and moves real money there, so the differentiation argument cannot be geographic. What it does not do is serve organisations that lack formal registration, two years of financial statements, a documented board, a prior-funder track record, and the administrative capacity to complete a due diligence application inside 21 days. Every one of those requirements is a reasonable fraud control, and together they define an eligibility floor. A community flood-response group formed eighteen months ago is below that floor, and it is below it because of documentation rather than legitimacy. That is the gap Fund the Future occupies: unserved organisations inside well-served countries.

**[Inference]** The corollary is uncomfortable and belongs in the product brief. Every requirement Fund the Future removes to reach those organisations is a fraud control GlobalGiving chose to keep. The product's differentiator and its largest risk are the same design decision, and the verification ladder has to be good enough to substitute for controls it is deliberately dropping rather than simply dropping them.

**[Inference]** GlobalGiving's 7% plus 3% for non-US/UK organisations, with a 15% ceiling on total deductions, is the benchmark a Fund the Future fee has to be read against. Combined with the stacked cross-border cost estimated above, a platform fee that pushes total leakage past roughly 10% offers a worse deal than an established platform with a stronger trust proposition, which constrains monetization more tightly than "small fee" implies.

### Other platforms

**[Fact]** GoFundMe: no platform fee, funded by optional donor tips, 2.9% plus US$0.30 processing (2.2% for verified 501(c)(3) charities). (Source: [Zeffy](https://www.zeffy.com/compare/chuffed-vs-gofundme).)

**[Fact]** Chuffed publishes three pricing models, of which the default, Keep 100, charges **0% platform fee**, passes payment processing to the donor and funds Chuffed through an optional donor contribution. Chuffed states that optional donations currently supply 46% of its own funding, the remainder being investment and philanthropic or government grants. (Source: primary, [Chuffed pricing](https://chuffed.org/pricing), verified 21 August 2026. The 4.9% fallback recorded in an earlier pass does not appear on Chuffed's own page and should not be quoted.)

**[Fact]** Donorbox: platform fee of 2.95% on the free plan, 1.75% on Pro at US$150 per month, 1.6% on Premium, plus processing pass-through at nonprofit rates. (Same source.)

**[Assumption]** No fee or coverage data was found for Every.org, M-Changa, Ketto, or Milaap. Ketto and Milaap are India-focused and Fund the Future's pilot markets no longer include India, so their relevance has dropped. M-Changa is Kenya-focused and similarly less relevant after the market change. Every.org remains worth checking.

**[Inference]** The fee landscape splits into two models: a percentage fee on funds raised (GlobalGiving, Donorbox), and a donor-tip model that shifts the cost from the organisation to the donor (GoFundMe, Chuffed). Fund the Future's monetization was subsequently reversed from the first model to the second (locked decision 5 in [`scope.md`](scope.md)), for the reasons the stacked cost analysis above sets out. Chuffed's disclosure that tips supply 46% of its funding is the closest available evidence on how far a tip model actually carries an organisation, and it says: not the whole way.

## 7. Verification and donor trust

**[Fact]** GoFundMe states that fraudulent campaigns are less than 0.1% of campaigns posted, with its CEO putting misuse at under one tenth of one percent. (Sources: [The Hill](https://thehill.com/blogs/blog-briefing-room/news/437958-gofundme-ceo-says-less-than-1-percent-of-campaigns-are-fraud/), [GoFundMe safety page](https://www.gofundme.com/c/safety/fraudulent-campaigns).)

**[Inference]** This figure is self-reported by the platform and measures detected fraud on a platform whose campaigns are overwhelmingly domestic and personal, often shared within networks where the beneficiary is known to donors. Neither condition holds for Fund the Future, where the donor and the organisation are in different countries and share no social graph. The 0.1% number should not be carried across as a baseline expectation.

**[Fact]** GoFundMe has refunded all contributors to a campaign that prosecutors alleged defrauded donors of more than US$400,000, and platforms with such guarantees typically refund only after an internal investigation confirms fraud and the donor requests the refund. (Sources: [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/after-new-jersey-scam-gofundme-says-it-has-answer-fraud-n992086), [ACM research on crowdfunding fraud detection](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3501247.3531541).)

**[Inference]** A refund guarantee is only affordable if the platform controls the money long enough to claw it back or holds reserves against the risk. Under the architecture that avoids money transmitter classification, where funds settle directly to the organisation, a refund guarantee becomes an unsecured liability of the platform. The regulatory choice in section 4 and the trust promise in this section are the same decision viewed from two sides, and they cannot be made independently.

**[Fact]** A survey experiment with 1,200 participants found that signals of both financial and performance transparency significantly increase potential donors' willingness to donate and their intended donation amount, with donors more sensitive to financial transparency signals. (Source: [Public Performance & Management Review](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15309576.2025.2465750).)

**[Fact]** Research on certification labels and seals of approval is described in the literature as narrow and contradictory. Donors are willing to reward credible signals and accredited nonprofits achieve higher trust, but individuals often do not pay attention to the labels that voluntary programmes use as signalling devices. (Source: [Journal of Nonprofit & Public Sector Marketing](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10495142.2021.1954131).)

**[Inference]** This is the most directly actionable finding for the verification pillar and it argues against the obvious design. A verification badge is the cheapest thing to build and the evidence for badges specifically is weak and mixed, while the evidence for financial transparency is stronger and more consistent. A product whose trust mechanism is a green tick is building the less-supported signal. Showing where money goes appears to matter more to donors than showing that someone checked the organisation, which points the roadmap toward budget breakdowns and spend reporting rather than toward accreditation marks.

**[Inference]** There is a tension worth stating plainly: financial transparency is exactly what the target organisation is least equipped to provide, since it is the same documentation burden that keeps them off GlobalGiving. The verification design problem is therefore to produce credible financial transparency from organisations without accounting capacity, rather than to design a badge.

### What can actually be checked in the pilot markets

**[Fact]** Philippines: SEC register of non-stock corporations, with annual General Information Sheets and audited financial statements on file. (Source as section 3.)

**[Fact]** Nigeria: CAC register of Incorporated Trustees. A public name search exists. (Source as section 3.)

**[Fact]** Honduras: URSAC register under the 2011 NGO-D Law. (Source as section 3.)

**[Assumption]** None of the three registries was confirmed to offer machine-readable or programmatic lookup. No API, bulk data export, or structured search was found for any of them in this research.

**[Inference]** If that assumption holds, automated registry verification is not available in any pilot market, and verification is manual document review performed by a human. That makes verification a variable cost per organisation rather than a fixed platform cost, and it collides directly with the locked monetization model. A percentage fee on funds raised earns the platform very little from a small organisation raising a small amount, while costing the same manual review as a large one. Either verification depth scales with amount raised, which is the tiered ladder the scope anticipated, or the unit economics fail at the small end, which is the end the product exists to serve. This should be verified before it is treated as settled, since a registry API in even one market would change the design.

## 8. Runtime inputs: what the product actually needs

The core promise is that a small amount of entered information becomes a complete campaign page with a working donation path. Aggregate research figures do not establish what a single campaign creation actually requires, so the inputs are listed separately here.

**To generate the page**
| Input | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Organisation name, location, contact | Self-reported | **[Fact]** trivially available |
| Mission and campaign narrative | Self-reported, platform-assisted drafting | **[Assumption]** most target organisations can describe their work but not write fundraising copy |
| Funding goal and what it buys | Self-reported | **[Assumption]** unvalidated by the platform, and a goal with no cost breakdown weakens the financial-transparency signal that section 7 shows donors respond to |
| Imagery | Self-supplied photos | **[Assumption]** most target organisations have no marketing assets, and this is a real drop-off risk at page creation |

**To move money (imposed by the payment provider, not by the platform)**
| Input | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Legal entity status and registration number | Registry document upload | **[Fact]** required by every payment provider's onboarding |
| Bank or mobile-money payout destination | Self-reported, name-matched against legal name | **[Inference]** name matching is the cheapest high-value fraud control available |

**[Inference]** The most important structural point is that the payment provider's KYC, not the platform's verification ladder, is what actually gates money movement. The platform can add checks on top and can present them to donors as trust signals, but it cannot reduce below the provider's floor. Any promise that an organisation can go from signup to receiving money in minutes is constrained by provider onboarding, which is typically days. The realistic product shape is that page creation is instant and payout activation is not, and the campaign page needs to work, and be shareable, during the gap.

**[Assumption]** No provider onboarding timelines were verified for any pilot market. This should be established before the product brief claims any end-to-end speed.

## 9. Monetization

**[Fact]** Benchmarks established above: GlobalGiving at 7% plus 3% for non-US/UK organisations with a 15% ceiling on total deductions; Donorbox at 2.95% plus processing on its free tier; GoFundMe and Chuffed at 0% platform fee funded by donor tips.

**[Inference]** A percentage fee on funds raised is defensible only if total leakage stays clearly below GlobalGiving's, because GlobalGiving offers more service, more trust, and 175-country coverage. Given cross-border costs of 7% to 12% before any platform fee, the room available is narrow, possibly 2% to 3%.

**[Inference]** The variable cost of manual verification, per section 7, is the platform's main per-organisation cost and is largely independent of amount raised. A percentage fee therefore recovers verification cost well from large campaigns and poorly from small ones. This is a direct conflict between the monetization model and the target user, and it is a stronger argument against fee-on-funds than the fairness objection.

**[Inference]** The donor-tip model resolves both problems: it moves the cost to the better-resourced party and decouples revenue from campaign size. Its weakness is revenue unpredictability.

**Decision (user, August 2026): donor tips. The organisation pays nothing.** This overturns the fee-on-funds model locked at kickoff, on the evidence in this section. All platform revenue now depends on voluntary donor top-ups, which makes tip uptake the assumption the business model rests on entirely.

**[Inference]** US international affairs giving of $35.54bn in 2024 against ~$82bn of global cross-border philanthropy from 47 countries in 2023 puts the US at roughly two-fifths of the world total. Two independently produced figures agreeing at this level raises confidence in both.

### What this establishes for the product

**[Inference]** Two consequences follow, and each one lands directly on a decision in the product brief.

1. **The product is not redistributing a large existing flow.** Cross-border philanthropy is roughly 11% of global individual charitable giving, and about 3.6% of the $2.3tn all-sources money-and-time total. ## 11. How money moves through the philanthropy sector

Section 10 sized the flows. This section describes the mechanism that carries them, because the product's core proposition is to shorten that mechanism.

### The chain

**[Fact]** Philanthropic intermediaries are entities that regrant philanthropic money, and include fiscal sponsors, community foundations, pooled and collaborative funds, donor-advised funds, giving circles and regranting organisations. (Source: [PEAK Grantmaking](https://www.peakgrantmaking.org/insights/an-introduction-to-intermediaries/), [Inside Philanthropy](https://www.insidephilanthropy.com/explainers/what-is-a-philanthropic-intermediary).)

**[Fact]** A donor-advised fund is a US charitable vehicle where the donor deposits cash or securities, surrenders legal ownership in exchange for an immediate tax deduction, and retains advisory rights over how the balance is invested and when it is distributed. DAFs move billions of dollars a year into the nonprofit sector as an intermediate step. (Source: [Penn SP2 working paper](https://sp2.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Heist-Vance-McMullen_Understanding-Donor-Advised-Funds_working-paper-002.pdf), with structural description corroborated by [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donor-advised_fund). Search-snippet sourced. **Weak sourcing:** the mechanism is uncontroversial and widely documented, but no primary regulatory or IRS source was consulted, and no DAF volume figure is quoted here for that reason.)

**[Inference]** A dollar given by an individual in a high-income country and spent by a community group in a low-income country typically passes through some sequence of: a giving vehicle such as a DAF, a grantmaking foundation, an international NGO, a national affiliate, and finally the local implementer. Each hop imposes its own eligibility requirements, reporting obligations and administrative cost, and each hop is a point at which an organisation without formal documentation is filtered out. This is the structural explanation for section 10's narrowing chain and for the 4.5% direct-funding figure in humanitarian aid.

**[Fact]** Some intermediaries offer fiscal sponsorship, which allows an organisation that is not itself registered to receive charitable funding under another entity's legal status. Regranting funds such as the CLIMA Fund and Global Greengrants Fund exist specifically to move money to grassroots climate groups. (Source: [Alliance magazine](https://www.alliancemagazine.org/blog/localising-philanthropy-what-does-it-entail/), [CAF America](https://cafamerica.org/blog/challenges-of-localization/). Search-snippet sourced.)

**[Inference]** Fiscal sponsorship is the existing answer to the problem this product addresses, and it works. Its limits are that it requires finding a willing sponsor, it adds a fee layer, and it makes the sponsor rather than the organisation the accountable party to the donor. Fiscal sponsorship is therefore the closest existing substitute for what this product does, and the benchmark any speed or directness claim should be measured against.

**[Inference]** Every intermediary in the chain performs a control function as well as a cost function. Section 6 established the same point about GlobalGiving's eligibility floor. Shortening the chain therefore transfers control work to the platform rather than eliminating it, and section 14 is where that cost has to be met.

## 12. Why people give, and how much

### The behavioural evidence

**[Fact]** The identifiable victim effect is the tendency to give more when an appeal names and depicts a specific individual than when it describes an unidentified group, and the effect is mediated by affect rather than reasoning. In a neuroimaging study, subjects gave more to orphans shown in photographs than in silhouettes, and nucleus accumbens activity statistically accounted for the increase. (Source: [Journal of Neuroscience](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/33/43/17188.full).)

**[Fact]** Warm glow describes donors deriving utility from the act of giving itself rather than only from the outcome produced. Happier people give more, and giving more increases reported happiness. (Source: [Global Council for Behavioral Science](https://gc-bs.org/articles/behavioral-economics-in-charitable-giving-motivations-and-barriers/). Search-snippet sourced.)

**[Fact]** Overhead aversion is well documented, and framing administrative cost positively, for example "90% goes directly to programs", yields more donations than disclosing the same cost negatively. (Same source.)

**[Inference]** These three findings point the campaign page in a consistent direction: a named person, a specific and bounded project, and a positive framing of where the money goes. This is favourable for the product, because a small local organisation with one identifiable leader and one concrete project is closer to the ideal appeal structure than a large NGO with a diversified programme portfolio. Scale is a disadvantage in this particular respect, and the target user has the advantage.

**[Inference]** Overhead aversion is a direct constraint on the fee decision in section 9. A platform fee is overhead from the donor's point of view, and the stacked cross-border cost of 8% to 12% is invisible to the donor but real to the organisation. How the page presents total deductions is a design decision with revenue consequences.

Small nonprofits raised $6.15 per email contact against $0.88 for large nonprofits. (Source: [Nonprofit Tech for Good](https://www.nptechforgood.com/101-best-practices/online-fundraising-statistics-for-nonprofits/). Search-snippet sourced, not upgraded.)

**[Fact]** Donors giving $1 to $100 were 57.0% of all donors in Q1 2025 and that group fell 11.1% year on year. (Source: [AFP Fundraising Effectiveness Project](https://afpglobal.org/news/fundraising-effectiveness-project-data-q1-2025-shows-increases-dollars-raised-declining). Search-snippet sourced. US-centric, applied here as a sector proxy.)

**[Inference] The environmental donor gives less and stays longer, and that combination points at the product's design.** On every retention measure the Environmental cut beats the all-cause average, and on every gift-size measure it falls below it, by a third on the average one-time gift. A product whose revenue depends on donor tips is therefore working with a donor who contributes smaller amounts per act and returns more reliably, which favours recurring giving and a second campaign over a single large push. Monthly giving already being 32% of environmental online revenue, the highest share of any sector M+R reports except Public Media, is the strongest single argument in this research for shipping recurring gifts in the MVP rather than deferring them.

**[Inference]** Three further consequences. First, a 1.6% visitor-to-donor conversion rate means fundraising outcomes are dominated by traffic volume, which sharpens riskiest assumption 1 in [`scope.md`](scope.md): a builder plus a link puts the entire traffic problem on an organisation that has no audience. Second, 24% new-donor retention across all causes, and 28% for environmental, means a single campaign raises money roughly once. Third, small organisations out-earn large ones per contact by a factor of seven, which is evidence that small lists are more responsive and that the target user's disadvantage is list size rather than list quality.

**[Inference]** December concentration is a planning constraint the product inherits. If 37% of online revenue arrives in one month, a campaign launched in February is competing for the thinnest part of the year, and payout onboarding of 5 to 10 business days has to be complete before December rather than started in it.

**[Assumption]** These benchmarks describe US nonprofits with existing digital programmes, measured through M+R's client base. They are applied here to Philippine organisations with no list and no prior programme, which is a different population. The direction of the environmental-versus-all-cause differences is more transferable than the levels.

## 13. The climate and environment slice, and the five-year mismatch

### How much philanthropy goes to climate

**[Fact]** Total climate mitigation funding from foundations and individuals reached an estimated $11.7bn to $18.4bn in 2024, a record, representing about 2.1% of total global philanthropic giving and crossing the 2% threshold for the first time. (Source: [ClimateWorks Foundation, Funding Trends](https://www.climateworks.org/report/funding-trends-2026/).)

**[Fact]** Foundation funding specifically for climate adaptation and resilience reached an estimated **$870m in 2024**, a historic high and more than double the $404m tracked in 2021. The number of foundations making adaptation-related grants grew 55%. (Source: [ClimateWorks, Foundation funding for climate change adaptation and resilience 2025](https://www.climateworks.org/press-release/funding-for-climate-adaptation-and-resilience-hits-record-of-870-million/), published 11 November 2025.)

**[Fact]** Less than 10% of total climate finance supports adaptation. (Source: [ClimateWorks](https://www.climateworks.org/report/funding-trends-2026/).)

**[Fact]** Asia and Oceania received less than 10% of adaptation funding between 2021 and 2024, despite being home to more than half the world's population. By sector, disaster risk management and infrastructure received the least funding over that period. (Same ClimateWorks adaptation source, verified primary 21 August 2026 along with the $870m, $404m and 55% figures.)

**[Inference]** The last finding is the single strongest evidence for the Philippines pilot in this document. The pilot market sits in the region that is both most exposed by population and least served by adaptation philanthropy, and the work most common among the target organisations, which is flood response, early warning and community infrastructure, falls in the sector categories ClimateWorks identifies as least funded. The gap is specific and nameable rather than general.

### The mismatch over the next five years

**[Fact]** Adaptation finance needs in developing countries are projected at $310bn per year by 2035 on modelled costs, rising to $365bn on needs extrapolated from Nationally Determined Contributions and National Adaptation Plans. International public adaptation finance was $26bn in 2023, down from $28bn in 2022. Needs are 12 to 14 times current flows, and the Glasgow Climate Pact doubling goal will be missed. (Source: primary, [UNEP Adaptation Gap Report 2025](https://www.unep.org/resources/adaptation-gap-report-2025), verified 21 August 2026. See section 1.)

**[Fact]** ODA from OECD DAC members fell 23.1% in real terms in 2025 to $174.3bn, the largest annual decline on record. (Source: [OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2026/04/international-aid-fell-sharply-in-2025-says-oecd.html), cited in full in section 10.)

**[Inference]** Put the two sides together and the arithmetic is stark. Philanthropic adaptation funding of $870m sits against a projected annual need of $310bn, which is **0.28%**. Over the next five years the need rises toward the 2035 projection while both of its current funding sources contract: international public adaptation finance fell between 2022 and 2023, and total ODA fell 23.1% in 2025. The gap widens from both directions at once.

**[Note, 22 August 2026]** The comparisons in this subsection set a 2035 need against a 2024 or 2023 flow. That is UNEP's own construction and it stays here, because this document is backing material and should hold the figures in the form the source published them. Public-facing writing uses the annualised gap in section 1 instead, per the user's framing decision.

**[Inference]** Philanthropy cannot close a $310bn gap and the product brief must not imply otherwise. What the numbers do support is a narrower and defensible claim: adaptation philanthropy is small, growing fast, more than doubling in three years, and structurally misallocated away from the region and the sectors where exposure is highest. A product that increases the number of fundable organisations and the number of people giving to them is acting on the growth and the misallocation, which is a real contribution at a scale a platform can plausibly affect.

**[Inference]** This is also where the pie-growth goal earns its evidence. Adaptation philanthropy more than doubled between 2021 and 2024 and the funder count grew 55%, so this is a category in expansion rather than a fixed pot being divided. Growing it further is consistent with the observed trend rather than contrary to it.

## 14. The small-organisation problem: trust, effort, and transparency

### Why small organisations struggle to raise

**[Inference]** Combining findings above, a small local organisation faces three compounding disadvantages. It sits below the documentation floor that intermediaries and platforms use for eligibility (section 6 and section 11). It has no audience, and conversion at 1.6% of visitors means outcomes track traffic (section 12). It is unknown to the donor, and the donor is being asked to send money across a border to a name they cannot verify (section 7).

**[Fact]** Small nonprofits raise $6.15 per email contact against $0.88 for large nonprofits (section 12 source).

**[Inference]** The third disadvantage is therefore not matched by a proportional disadvantage in donor responsiveness. Small organisations convert their contacts far better than large ones. The binding constraint is reach and credibility rather than appeal quality, which supports building for distribution and trust rather than for better campaign copy.

### What the evidence says about transparency

**[Fact]** Researchers at Villanova University and the University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee found that nonprofits earning a GuideStar Seal of Transparency averaged 53% more in contributions the following year than organisations without one. Candid's later study reports a 62% increase. Profiles with Gold or Platinum seals receive twice the views, and contributions to them were 11% larger than to Bronze or Silver seal holders. (Source: [Candid](https://learn.guidestar.org/transparency), [Candid study summary](https://candid.org/blogs/study-confirms-nonprofit-transparency-candid-seals-boost-donor-contributions/).)

**Source-quality warning, and a conflict with section 7.** **[Inference]** These figures need three caveats before use. Candid operates the seal and publishes the research demonstrating its value, so the source is not disinterested. The design is observational, and organisations that choose to complete a transparency profile plausibly differ from those that do not on capability, staffing and professionalism, so selection effects could account for much of the measured difference. And the seals rate disclosure completeness rather than verified accuracy, so what is being measured is willingness to publish rather than truthfulness.

**[Inference]** With those caveats the evidence still points the same way as section 7's finding that financial-transparency signals outperform certification badges, and it strengthens it. The mechanism that appears to work is publishing structured, specific information about the organisation and its money. The mechanism with weak and contradictory support is displaying a third-party mark of approval. These are different product features and the brief should treat them separately.

**[Inference]** The evidence therefore separates two things that are easily conflated. Audited financial statements are what the target organisation cannot produce, and section 6 established that as the barrier at GlobalGiving. Structured disclosure, meaning a named accountable person, a line-by-line account of what the money buys, a record of what the organisation has done before, and reporting after the fact, is a different artefact, and it is the one the transparency evidence actually measures. Whether the product should be built on structured disclosure is a decision for the brief.

**[Inference]** Section 12's behavioural evidence points at the same artefact from a different direction. The identifiable victim effect favours a named person and a specific bounded project, and the transparency evidence favours a named accountable person and a costed budget. Two independent bodies of evidence converge on the same page contents, which is the strongest internal consistency in this research.

### How hard it currently is to start

**[Fact]** GlobalGiving requires formal registration, two years of financial statements, a documented board and a prior-funder track record (section 6). Payment providers independently require legal entity status, registration number, identity documents for a responsible person, beneficial ownership and a name-matched payout account (section 8).

**[Inference]** The floor is therefore set twice, by the platform and by the payment provider, and the provider's floor cannot be lowered by any product decision. The realistic product shape stated in section 8 holds: page creation can be immediate and payout activation cannot. The honest version of the simplicity claim is that the product removes the platform's floor and makes the provider's floor navigable, rather than that it removes documentation requirements altogether.

**[Assumption]** No measured data was found on how long it takes a small organisation in any market to go from deciding to fundraise online to receiving a first donation, on any existing platform. This is the benchmark the product's speed claim would be measured against and it is currently unavailable.

## 15. Philippine verification and onboarding mechanics

Added August 2026, after the pilot narrowed to the Philippines and the user decided that the campaign page shows verification evidence rather than a badge. That decision made the question of what can actually be retrieved from the SEC register load-bearing rather than theoretical.

### What the SEC register returns, free and paid

**[Fact]** The free public search, available through eSPARC, the SEC eSEARCH facility and the official SEC Check App, accepts a company name or an SEC registration number and returns the company's **status, address, and record of reportorial submissions, including General Information Sheets and Audited Financial Statements**. Basic searches require no account. Status values include Registered, Revoked, Suspended and Dissolved, alongside the registered name, SEC number and date of incorporation. (Source: [Respicio & Co. commentary](https://www.respicio.ph/commentaries/how-to-check-if-a-company-is-legally-registered-with-the-sec-in-the-philippines) and [Respicio & Co. verification guide](https://www.lawyer-philippines.com/articles/how-to-verify-sec-registration-of-a-philippine-corporation-online).)

**[Fact]** The documents themselves are a paid service. Certified copies of GIS, AFS and Articles of Incorporation are obtained through the SEC Express System for a fee, delivered by courier or digital download, and are certified by the Commission as definitive proof of status. (Same sources.)

**[Fact]** "Revoked" status commonly results from failure to file GIS and AFS. (Same source.)

**[Inference]** This is the most favourable possible answer for the product, and it is worth being explicit about why. The verification decision requires the page to show that an organisation is registered and that it has filed. Both of those are in the free tier. The paid tier holds the certified documents, which the platform does not need in order to make its display honest, and which a sceptical donor can obtain independently. The platform can therefore state a checkable fact without becoming a document broker.

**[Inference]** The revocation mechanism adds a second signal at no cost. Because non-filing leads to revocation, a current status of Registered is itself weak evidence of continuing compliance, not merely of past existence. This is exactly the property the Philippines was selected for in [`market-selection.md`](market-selection.md), now confirmed rather than assumed.

**[Fact] The API exists, and it is priced. Answered 21 August 2026.** The SEC runs an API Marketplace at [portal.sec.gov.ph](https://portal.sec.gov.ph/) offering two products. **Company Information Lookup** is a bundle of nine APIs returning "registered business names, official business addresses, SEC numbers, registration status, secondary licences, AFS, GIS and many more", sold as Package A at **PHP 10,000 for 100 API calls per year** or Package B at **PHP 50,000 for 1,000 calls per year**, with calls cut off once the limit is reached until renewal. A separate **SEC Number** API is **free at 10 API calls per day** and retrieves company data using the SEC number as the parameter. Both are subscription products behind a developer portal with multi-factor authentication and a published subscription agreement. (Source: primary, portal.sec.gov.ph, read 21 August 2026.)

**[Inference]** Verification can be automated, and its unit cost is now a known number rather than an unknown. Package A works out at PHP 100 per call and Package B at PHP 50, roughly US$1.75 and US$0.90 at an assumed PHP 57 to the dollar (**[Assumption]** on the exchange rate, which is not part of the SEC's published pricing). That is the cost of machine-checking one organisation's status and filing record, before any staff time.

**[Inference]** The free tier is the more interesting one at pilot scale. Ten calls a day is 3,650 a year, which comfortably exceeds the number of organisations an MVP would onboard, and it is free. The catch is that the free product is keyed on the SEC number and its exact response fields are not documented publicly, so whether it returns the filing history the verification display depends on, or only identity fields, cannot be confirmed without an account. **The remaining task is to register for the free tier and inspect one response**, which is a small, concrete task rather than an open research question.

**[Inference]** The cost question that mattered is settled either way. Under the donor-tip model the platform earns nothing from the organisation, so verification is a fixed operating cost rather than a per-campaign margin question. At PHP 50 to 100 per paid call it stays immaterial at pilot scale and becomes a real line item only at thousands of campaigns, by which point the free tier or a negotiated arrangement is worth pursuing.

**A second, independent verification signal.** **[Fact]** The Philippine Council for NGO Certification, a private non-stock body that certifies NGOs against minimum financial-management and accountability criteria, publishes a **public register of its 604 accredited organisations** showing sector category, BIR certificate expiry date and PCNC certificate expiry date. Accreditation runs for one, three or five years depending on evaluation outcome, and is a prerequisite for registration with the Bureau of Internal Revenue as a qualified donee institution, which confers full deductibility of donations. (Source: primary, [PCNC accreditation](https://pcnc.com.ph/accreditation/) and [register of accredited NGOs](https://pcnc.com.ph/accredited-ngos/), read 21 August 2026.)

**[Inference]** PCNC is free to check, carries an expiry date rather than a permanent badge, and means something specific: an independent body inspected this organisation's financial management within a defined window. For the small minority of campaign creators who hold it, the campaign page can show it as a dated fact alongside the SEC record. For the majority who do not, its absence must carry no implication, since the product exists precisely for organisations below that bar. Displayed as "accredited by PCNC until <date>" it fits locked decision 6's evidence-not-badge rule; displayed as a tick or a trust score it would violate it.

### Payment provider onboarding timelines

**[Fact]** Xendit reviews KYC submissions within **5 to 10 business days**. E-wallet activation responses arrive within 5 business days, QR partner registration within 7 business days, and virtual accounts activate automatically once KYC completes. (Source: [Xendit Help Center](https://help.xendit.co/hc/en-us/articles/11103239008793-PH-What-are-the-legal-documents-required-to-register-to-Xendit-for-Philippine-Merchants).)

**[Fact] Partially answered 21 August 2026.** dLocal publishes no approval SLA, and its self-serve arm's terms establish the shape of the process instead: a merchant cannot receive funds until KYC completes, and **if KYC is not completed within 30 days of the account opening the account is deactivated** and processing stops. dLocal reserves the right to reject an application during or after onboarding on KYC results, risk assessment "or any other internal criteria, without being required to provide reasons". Required documentation includes identity verification, a taxpayer or national identification number, and supporting anti-money-laundering documentation. (Source: primary, [dLocal Go terms and conditions](https://dlocalgo.com/en/terms-and-conditions), read 21 August 2026.)

**[Inference]** The absence of a published review time, next to a published 30-day cutoff, describes a process that is discretionary at the front and unforgiving at the back. For the product this is a support obligation rather than a technical one: an organisation that stalls on documentation loses its account rather than merely waiting, so onboarding needs chasing built into it. The unexplained-rejection clause also means some verified, legitimate organisations will be refused a payout rail for reasons the platform cannot see or appeal, which is a failure mode the campaign page has to survive.

**[Inference]** This puts a number on the constraint section 8 identified without one. An organisation can create and share a campaign page immediately, and cannot receive money for one to two weeks. Any product claim about speed has to be stated against page creation rather than against money arriving, and the campaign page needs to work and be shareable during the gap. That is a product requirement rather than a caveat.

## 17. Climate exposure, climate anxiety, and what turns concern into a gift

Added 22 August 2026 at the user's request, to test whether the spread of climate experience is itself an opportunity for the product, and whether the distress that comes with it connects to giving.

### How widely climate change is being experienced

**[Fact]** Analysis of nationally representative survey data from more than 128,000 people across 142 countries finds that people who have lived through floods, heatwaves or other climate-related disasters are significantly more likely to rate climate change a very serious threat. (Source: [reporting on the study](https://phys.org/news/2025-10-extreme-weather-climate-perceptions-worldwide.html). Search-snippet sourced; the underlying journal article was not read in this pass.)

**[Fact]** In a 2024 global survey across 77 countries, 43% thought extreme weather events were worse than the year before. (Source: [Clean Energy Wire factsheet on global climate surveys](https://www.cleanenergywire.org/factsheets/global-surveys-show-peoples-growing-concern-about-climate-change). Search-snippet sourced.)

**[Fact]** Hickman et al., published in *The Lancet Planetary Health* in December 2021, surveyed 10,000 people aged 16 to 25 across ten countries including Brazil, India and Nigeria. 59% were very or extremely worried about climate change, between 50% and 67% reported sadness, fear, anxiety, anger, powerlessness, helplessness or guilt, 45% said those feelings negatively affected daily functioning including eating, sleeping and studying, and 75% agreed that the future is frightening. Distress was significantly associated with perceived inadequacy of government response. (Source: [The Lancet Planetary Health](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanplh/article/PIIS2542-5196(21)00278-3/fulltext). Abstract-level, the full paper was not read.)

**[Fact]** Gallup records a high of 48% of Americans calling global warming a serious threat, and separate US polling puts the share saying they have personally experienced the effects of global warming at 47% in late 2025. (Sources: [Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/659387/record-high-call-global-warming-serious-threat.aspx), [Climate Change in the American Mind, Fall 2025](https://climatecommunication.gmu.edu/all/climate-change-in-the-american-mind-beliefs-attitudes-fall-2025/). Search-snippet sourced.)

### Whether that concern converts into giving

**[Fact]** In experimental work on media coverage, exposure to global-warming news increased state eco-anxiety and increased donations to environmental organisations, while the study found no significant link between eco-anxiety itself and pro-environmental behaviour. (Source: [Journal of Environmental Psychology](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272494423001780). Search-snippet sourced, abstract only.)

**[Fact]** Climate anxiety produces pro-environmental behaviour in some people and eco-paralysis in others, with self-efficacy acting as the mediating variable. (Source: [study on climate anxiety, pro-environmental behaviour and general self-efficacy](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9960236/).)

**[Fact]** Hope and despair moderate that relationship. Lower despair strengthens the link between climate anxiety and pro-environmental action, and higher hope strengthens it too. Where eco-anxiety produces helplessness, the person's sense of being able to change anything falls, and that becomes a barrier to acting. (Sources: [Environment and Behavior](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00139165241297050), [correlational meta-analysis](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0760/15/2/88), [climate anxiety across 32 countries](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0272494422001323). Search-snippet sourced.)

**[Inference]** Hopelessness is not a fundraising asset. The evidence runs the other way: despair suppresses the action that anxiety might otherwise produce, and efficacy is what carries it through. In the one experiment here that raised donations, the trigger was news exposure rather than the anxiety itself, so what a platform can act on is the availability of a specific credible thing to do at the moment concern is felt.

**[Inference]** This is a third independent line of evidence arriving at the same page design as section 12's identifiable-victim effect and section 14's transparency findings. A bounded project with a countable output, a costed budget and a named accountable person describes a specific act with a visible result, which is what an efficacy-mediated donation requires. The earlier two bodies of evidence explain what makes a donor trust the page. This one explains why the donor acts at all.

**[Inference]** It also gives the grow-the-pie goal in section 13 a mechanism rather than an aspiration. If concern is widespread and rising while its conversion into giving is limited by having nowhere credible to put it, then new money is available in principle to a product that supplies the missing half. This remains a mechanism rather than a measurement, and nothing here sizes it.

### Two findings that cut against the product

**[Fact]** About 80% of charitable giving in response to a disaster happens in the first 72 hours, and in one year studied only 2% of donors gave to recovery from disasters that had occurred in earlier years. (Source: [The Chronicle of Philanthropy](https://www.philanthropy.com/article/disaster-giving-goes-mostly-to-immediate-relief-not-prevention-or-long-term-recovery). Search-snippet sourced.)

**[Inference]** Adaptation has no 72-hour window. It is money spent before an event, with no news trigger and no date attached. The reflex that makes climate-related giving move fastest is the one reflex this product cannot use, which sharpens riskiest assumption 5 rather than answering it.

**[Fact]** A study of 1.23 million IRS Form 990 filings by US nonprofits between 2010 and 2021 found a significant positive relationship between climate risk and donations overall. The largest increases went to hospitals and human-service organisations, while internationally-focused nonprofits saw declines. (Source: [Journal of Business Ethics](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10551-025-06158-8). Search-snippet sourced, abstract only.)

**[Inference]** This is the sharpest counter-evidence in the project so far, because it describes the exact behaviour the product depends on and finds it moving the wrong way. Read plainly, as climate risk rises, US donors give closer to home and cross-border climate giving is the category that loses. Two things qualify it without dissolving it. The unit of analysis is the recipient organisation's IRS filing, so it measures US-registered internationally-focused nonprofits rather than direct cross-border gifts. And the period ends in 2021, before the adaptation-philanthropy growth recorded in section 13. It belongs in the product brief rather than in a footnote.

## 18. Where in the world the donors are

Added 22 August 2026, after the user reversed the decision to pilot with US donors only. The product position is now that a donor anywhere can give, so this section asks what the giving data supports and what it constrains.

### The global picture

**[Fact]** 60% of people globally donated money in 2025, and the global average donation is about 1% of income. By region, Africa gives the highest share of income at 1.6% and Europe the lowest at 0.6%. (Source: primary, [CAF World Giving Report 2026, donor insights](https://www.worldgivingreport.org/donor-insights), read 22 August 2026.)

**[Fact]** Giving splits three ways globally. Direct giving to people in need reaches 36% of people and carries 41% of donated value, charitable giving 34% and 38%, religious giving 23% and 21%. The Netherlands routes the highest share through charities at 79% and the lowest directly at 10%. Morocco is the highest direct giver at 77%. (Same source.)

**[Fact]** Ranked by share of income donated, the most generous countries in the 2025 edition were Nigeria at 2.83%, Egypt at 2.45%, and Ghana and China tied at 2.19%, against a global average of 1.04%. Indonesia gave 1.55% and ranked 21st of 101 countries surveyed, having previously led the index. (Sources: [Philanthropy Age](https://www.philanthropyage.org/articles/caf-world-giving-report-2025/), [Tempo](https://en.tempo.co/read/2035318/indonesia-slips-to-21st-place-among-worlds-most-generous-countries). Search-snippet sourced. Note the 1% and 1.04% averages come from different editions and are not a conflict.)

**[Fact]** Only 22% of donors globally support charities working across many countries, against 56% supporting charities working locally and 55% supporting national ones. Donors in higher-income countries give a higher share to multi-country charities than donors in lower-income countries. (Sources: [Civil Society coverage of the report](https://www.civilsociety.co.uk/news/uk-ranked-64th-most-generous-country-in-caf-world-giving-report.html), [PR Newswire release](https://www.prnewswire.co.uk/news-releases/world-giving-report-reveals-factors-that-increase-generosity-to-good-causes-302789605.html). Search-snippet sourced.)

**[Fact]** CAF's 2026 edition reports global donations falling year on year. (Source: [Dompet Dhuafa summary of the 2026 report](https://www.dompetdhuafa.org/en/world-giving-report-2026-indonesians-charity-remains-steady-as-global-donations-fall-with-72-per-cent-through-laz/). Search-snippet sourced.)

**[Inference]** The two headline measures point in opposite directions and both matter. The countries giving the largest share of their income are largely in Africa and Asia, and the countries most willing to give across a border are the high-income ones giving the smallest share. A product whose recipients sit in one country and whose donors sit anywhere lives at that intersection, and roughly one donor in five gives to work in another country at all. Cross-border giving is a minority behaviour in every market, including the generous ones.

**[Inference]** The charitable-versus-direct split is a selection signal in its own right. This product routes money to an organisation, so a market where giving flows person-to-person suits it less well than one where giving flows through charities, whatever either market's total generosity. The Netherlands at 79% charitable and Morocco at 77% direct are the two ends of that spectrum.

**[Inference]** Reach and acquisition are different claims and the brief should make them separately. Nothing in the architecture is donor-market-specific: card and wallet acceptance from most countries is a solved problem, and payout into the Philippines was the binding constraint, which this reversal does not touch. Acquisition is another matter, because the first donors will concentrate in a small number of markets.

**[Assumption]** A proposed starting set of donor markets is the United States, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, Germany, Canada and Australia, on high income, a high charitable share of giving, mature online donation behaviour and English or high English proficiency. No source ranks countries on cross-border giving specifically, so this is reasoning from CAF's regional and income patterns rather than a measured ranking. It should be tested rather than treated as settled.

**[Inference]** Two consequences follow elsewhere in this document. The M+R environmental benchmarks in section 12 are US data already being applied to Philippine organisations with no list, and applying them to a globally sourced donor base weakens them further, so riskiest assumption 8 needs restating rather than narrowing. And nothing about the payout side changes, because the constraint was always the recipient's rail rather than the donor's.

### Tax deductibility across donor markets

**[Fact]** In the United States, IRC section 170(c)(2) limits the charitable deduction to corporations, funds or foundations created or organised in the United States or under its laws. A gift made directly to a foreign charity is therefore not deductible for a US donor, whatever the recipient's standing at home. (Sources: [IRC section 170 text](https://irc.bloombergtax.com/public/uscode/doc/irc/section_170), [IRS guidance on charitable contribution deductions](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/charitable-organizations/charitable-contribution-deductions).)

**[Fact]** A US charity acting as a mere conduit does not produce a deduction either. Where the domestic organisation is required by its charter, bylaws or an agreement with the donor to transmit funds to a named foreign organisation, the deduction is disallowed. Deductibility requires the US entity to retain full control and discretion over the funds, with the gift not earmarked for a specific foreign recipient. (Sources: [Rev. Rul. 63-252](https://www.taxnotes.com/research/federal/irs-guidance/revenue-rulings/rev-rul-63-252/d5p2), [practitioner summary](https://fsb-law.com/tax-matters/charitable-contributions-to-foreign-organizations-andor-designated-to-foreign-organizations/). Search-snippet sourced.)

**[Inference]** Deductibility and the custody decision are one decision seen from two sides. Every route to a US deduction requires a US charitable entity that receives the money, holds it, and is genuinely free to decide not to send it onward. That is exactly the custody that locked decision 4 gave up to stay out of money transmitter licensing. The product cannot offer a US deduction without becoming the thing it declined to be, which makes this a structural trade rather than a missing feature. It is also the clearest statement available of what a fiscal sponsor sells that this product does not.

**[Fact]** In the European Union, the Court of Justice held in *Persche v Finanzamt Lüdenscheid* (2009) that a member state cannot refuse a deduction for a donation solely because the recipient is established in another member state, where that recipient meets the domestic criteria for charitable status. Member states remain free to set those criteria and are not required to recognise a foreign charitable status automatically. (Sources: [ECNL briefing](https://ecnl.org/news/briefing-academy-european-law-era-persche-case), [Withers](http://www.withersworldwide.com/news-publications/hein-persche-v-finanzamt-l%C3%BCdenscheid-cross-border-giving-within-eu-ramifications-for-charities-and-donors), [EFC study on cross-border philanthropy after Persche and Stauffer](https://efc.issuelab.org/resources/18545/18545.pdf). Search-snippet sourced.)

**[Assumption]** *Persche* addresses giving within the EU and EEA and does not extend the same treatment to gifts to third countries such as the Philippines, where member states may restrict relief on the grounds that no mutual administrative assistance exists for verifying the recipient. No primary source for the third-country position was read in this pass, and this should be verified before any public claim rests on it.

**[Assumption]** UK Gift Aid requires the recipient to be a charity recognised by HMRC, so a direct gift to a Philippine organisation does not qualify. Not verified against HMRC guidance in this pass.

**[Inference]** The practical position is the same in every market examined: a donor gets no tax relief for giving directly to a Philippine grassroots organisation through this product. Opening the donor market beyond the United States neither creates nor solves that problem, and the brief should state the position generally rather than as a US-specific caveat.

## 19. New philanthropic capital: corporate and founder pledges

Added 22 August 2026 at the user's request, to establish whether pledged corporate and founder wealth is relevant to this product and, if so, where.

**[Fact]** Pledge 1% asks companies to set aside 1% of equity, product, profit or employee time for social causes, and works with companies to reserve equity before a liquidity event. It counts more than 3,400 member companies across 85 countries, including Salesforce, Atlassian and Yelp, and reports unlocking over US$1bn in new philanthropy. The model originates with Salesforce setting aside 1% of its equity in 1999. (Source: [Pledge 1%](https://www.pledge1percent.org/pledge-1-unlocks-another-1-billion-in-new-philanthropy/). Search-snippet sourced. Membership counts are reported inconsistently across the organisation's own pages, from 3,400 to 8,500 depending on the page and date, so the lower figure is used here.)

**[Fact]** Anthropic's seven cofounders pledged 80% of their wealth to philanthropy in late January 2026, directed at the societal risks of advanced AI, particularly wealth concentration and economic disruption. At the valuation being discussed at the time this is estimated at US$39bn to US$59bn. The pledges are non-binding. Employees have separately pledged shares, with the company matching. (Sources: [Transformer](https://www.transformernews.ai/p/anthropic-employees-philanthropy-billions-donations-effective-altruism-coefficient-giving-ai-safety), [InvestmentNews](https://www.investmentnews.com/retirement-planning/wealthy-donors-stay-the-course-on-giving-as-anthropic-funnels-ai-fortunes-into-philanthropy/265452). Search-snippet sourced, no primary announcement read in this pass.)

**[Inference]** None of this is addressable market for this product, and treating it as such would repeat exactly the error section 10 warns about. Pledged equity converts through foundations and donor-advised funds, which section 11 describes, and it lands as institutional grants rather than as retail donations on a campaign page. The Anthropic pledge is also directed at a different problem.

**[Inference]** It is still worth carrying, in two places. As a why-now signal it evidences that new philanthropic capacity is forming at scale rather than that the existing pie is being redivided, which is the same directional argument as section 13's growth in adaptation funders. As a go-to-market route it is concrete: employee matching is the part of corporate pledge machinery that sends an individual's donation to an organisation the individual chose, which is the shape this product's donation path already has. Whether any pledge programme would match a donation to an unregistered-in-their-country recipient is untested and is the question that decides whether the route is real.

**[Assumption]** The employee-matching route is proposed on the mechanism rather than on evidence. No source examined describes a corporate matching programme handling a cross-border donation to a small foreign organisation, and matching programmes commonly restrict eligibility to domestic registered charities. This should be checked before the route is presented as available.

1. **Whether retail giving works for this sector at all.** In Kenya, the only market with a government-published breakdown, individual donors were 2.3% of NGO income in FY 2022/23 and fell 10% year on year, against affiliates at 27.6% and foreign government agencies at 21.2% (**[Fact]**, [Annual NGO Sector Report 2022/2023](https://www.pbora.go.ke/sites/default/files/2025-09/Annual%20NGOS%20Sector%20Report%202022-2023.pdf)). **Partially addressed, still open.** Sections 10 and 13 reframe it: retail giving to this sector is small because cross-border philanthropy as a whole is small, roughly 11% of global individual charitable giving, and because adaptation is a small share of climate philanthropy within that.
